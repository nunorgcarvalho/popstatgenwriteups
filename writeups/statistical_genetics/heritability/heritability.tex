\section{REML-based heritability estimation}
These derivations are based on the Methods of Yang et al. \citep{yang2010natgen}.

\subsection{Phenotype model}
We can define a quantitative phenotype vector $\by$ as:
$$ \by = \bX_c \bbeta + \bepsilon $$

Where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\by \in \R^N$: phenotypes.
    Centered so that $\E[\by]=0$.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $N$: number of samples.
    \end{itemize}
    \item $\bX_c \in \R^{N \times M_c}$: normalized genotypes for causal variants.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $M_c$: number of causal variants.
        \item Normalized according to
        $\bX_{c,i} = \frac{\bX'_{c,i} - 2 f_i}{\sqrt{2 f_i (1 - f_i)}}$.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item $\bX'_c \in \{0,1,2\}^{N \times M_c}$: allele dosages.
            \item $f_i$: true population allele frequency for variant $i$.
            \item Such that for each row (variant), $\E[\bX_{c,i}] = 0$ and $\Var[\bX_{c,i}] = 1$.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}
    \item $\bbeta \in \R^{M_c}$: per-normalized-genotype causal effects.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Assume infinitesimal model.
        \item Drawn from $\bbeta \sim \N(\bzero,\sigma_{\beta}^2 \bI )$.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item $\sigma_{\beta}^2$: variance of causal effects.
            \item $\bI \in \R^{M_c \times M_c}$: identity matrix.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}
    \item $\bepsilon \in \R^N$: residual effects (i.e. error or noise term).
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Drawn from $\bepsilon \sim \N(\bzero, \vareps \bI )$.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item $\vareps$: residual variance.
            \item $\bI \in \R^{N \times N}$: identity matrix.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
We assume $\bX_c$, $\bbeta$, and $\bepsilon$ are all independent from each other.

\subsection{Variance of the phenotype}
By making use of the independence between terms, we can define the variance-covariance matrix of $\by$ as:
\begin{align*}
    \Var[\by] &= \Var[\bX_c \bbeta + \bepsilon] \\
    &= \Var[\bX_c \bbeta] + \Var[\bepsilon] \\
    &= \Var[\bX_c] \Var[\bbeta] + \Var[\bepsilon] \\
    &= \sigma_{\beta}^2 (\bX_c \bX_c^{\T}) + \vareps \bI  \\
    &= M_c \sigma_{\beta}^2 \frac{\bX_c \bX_c^{\T}}{M_c}  + \vareps \bI  \\
    &= \varg \bG + \vareps \bI \\
\end{align*}
where we define $\bG = \frac{\bX_c \bX_c^{\T}}{M_c}$, with $\bG \in \R^{N \times N}$, as the genetic relationship matrix (GRM) between individuals.
The $G_{ii}$ element is the variance of individual $i$'s normalized genotype vector, while the $G_{ij}$ element is the covariance of individuals $i$ and $j$'s normalized genotype vectors.

We also define $\varg = M_c \sigma_{\beta}^2$ to be the variance of the total additive genetic effects on the phenotype. We can therefore think of individuals' phenotypes as being derived from the sum of a genetic random effect $\bg = \bX_c \bbeta$ and a residual random effect $\bepsilon$. From the general rule of multivariable statistics that $\Var[\bA \bv] = \bA \Var[\bv] \bA^{\T}$, we can write the genetic random effect as coming from a normal distribution:
$$ \bg \sim \N(\E[\bX \bbeta], \Var[\bX \bbeta]) $$
Because $\E[\bbeta] = \bzero$, then $\E[\bX \bbeta] = \bzero$. As for the variance, we can reuse the definitions from earlier, $\Var[\bX \bbeta] =  \bG \varg$. Therefore,
$$ \bg \sim \N(\bzero, \bG \varg) $$

Narrow-sense heritability is defined as the proportion of phenotypic variance, $\sigma_P^2$, explained by additive genetic effects:
$$ h^2 = \frac{\varg}{\sigma_P^2} = \frac{\varg}{\varg + \vareps} $$

\subsection{Estimating the GRM}

In practice, we likely do not know the exact set of causal variants and instead must estimate the GRM using a set of genotyped SNPs:

$$ \bA = \frac{\bX \bX^{\T}}{M} $$

Where $\bA$ is the estimated GRM, $\bX$ is the normalized genotype matrix of our genotyped SNPs, and $M$ is the number of genotyped SNPs.
Note that because we are also working with a sample, $X$ is normalized using sample allele frequencies, $\bp$:

$$ \bX_i = \frac{\bX'_i - 2 p_i}{\sqrt{2 p_i (1 - p_i)}} $$

However, this equation for $\bA$ ignores the sampling error associated with each SNP.
Let's consider the covariance computation between two individuals for SNP $i$, which is then summed across $M$ SNPs to get the value for $A_{jk}$.
When $j \neq k$:
\begin{align*}
    A_{ijk} &= x_{ij} x_{ik} \\
    &= \frac{x'_{ij} - 2 p_i}{\sqrt{2 p_i (1 - p_i)}}
    \frac{x'_{ik} - 2 p_i}{\sqrt{2 p_i (1 - p_i)}} \\
    &= \frac{(x'_{ij} - 2 p_i)(x'_{ik} - 2 p_i)}{2 p_i (1 - p_i)}
\end{align*}

Because $x'_{ij}$ and $x'_{ik}$ are independent from each other, the expected value of this is:

\begin{align*}
    \E[A_{ijk}] &= \E[\frac{(x'_{ij} - 2 p_i)(x'_{ik} - 2 p_i)}{2 p_i (1 - p_i)}] \\
    &= \frac{(\E[x'_{ij}] - 2 p_i)(\E[x'_{ik}] - 2 p_i)}{2 p_i (1 - p_i)} \\
    &= \frac{(2 p_i - 2 p_i)(2 p_i - 2 p_i)}{2 p_i (1 - p_i)} \\
    &= 0
\end{align*}
This makes sense if our sample is of unrelated individuals.
If the raw genotypes of two individuals at a SNP are independent from each other, then the covariance of their adjusted genotypes should also be zero.
Furthermore, the variance of $A_{ijk}$ is given by:
\begin{align*}
    \Var[A_{ijk}] &= \Var[\frac{(x'_{ij} - 2 p_i)(x'_{ik} - 2 p_i)}{2 p_i (1 - p_i)}] \\
    &= \frac{\Var[(x'_{ij} - 2 p_i)]\Var[(x'_{ik} - 2 p_i)]}{(2 p_i (1 - p_i))^2} \\
    &= \frac{\Var[(x'_{ij})]\Var[(x'_{ik})]}{(2 p_i (1 - p_i))^2} \\
    &= \frac{(2 p_i (2 - p_i))(2 p_i (2 - p_i))}{(2 p_i (1 - p_i))^2} \\
    &= 1
\end{align*}
So, the variance in $A_{jk}$ is independent of allele frequency, which is a desirable property.
But do these properties hold up when $j = k$ (i.e. variance of an indvidual's genotype)?
\begin{align*}
    A_{ijj} &= x_{ij}^2 \\
    &= (\frac{x'_{ij} - 2 p_i}{\sqrt{2 p_i (1 - p_i)}})^2 \\
    &= \frac{(x'_{ij} - 2 p_i)^2}{2 p_i (1 - p_i)}
\end{align*}
For deriving $\E[A_{ijj}]$, we make use of:
\begin{align*}
    E[{x'}_{ij}^2] &= \sum_{n=0}^{2} \E[{x'}_{ij}^2 | {x'}_{ij}=n] \Pr({x'}_{ij}=n) \\
    &= (0)^2 (1-p_i)^2 + (1)^2 2 p_i (1-p_i) + (2)^2 (p_i)^2 \\
    &= 2 p_i - 2 p_i^2 + 4 p_i^2 \\
    &= 2 p_i^2 + 2 p_i
\end{align*}
Allowing us to calculate $\E[A_{ijj}]$:
\begin{align*}
    \E[A_{ijj}] &= \E[\frac{(x'_{ij} - 2 p_i)^2}{2 p_i (1 - p_i)}] \\
    &= \frac{\E[{x'}_{ij}^2 - 4 p_i x'_{ij} + 4 p_i^2]}{2 p_i (1 - p_i)} \\
    &= \frac{\E[{x'}_{ij}^2] - 4 p_i \E[x'_{ij}] + 4 p_i^2}{2 p_i (1 - p_i)} \\
    &= \frac{2 p_i^2 + 2 p_i - 4 p_i (2 p_i) + 4 p_i^2}{2 p_i (1 - p_i)} \\
    &= \frac{2 p_i - 2 p_i^2}{2 p_i (1 - p_i)} \\
    &= \frac{2 p_i (1 - p_i)}{2 p_i (1 - p_i)} \\
    &= 1
\end{align*}
So, the expected variance of an individual's normalized genotype is 1 and independent of the allele frequency, which is a desirable property.
However, this frequency-independence does not hold for the variance.
For simplicity, let's denote $H = 2 p_i (1 - p_i)$ and make use of $\Var[Y] = \E[Y^2] - \E[Y]^2$:
\begin{align*}
    \Var[A_{ijj}] &= \Var[\frac{(x'_{ij} - 2 p_i)^2}{H}] \\
    &= \frac{\Var[(x'_{ij} - 2 p_i)^2]}{(H)^2} \\
    &= \frac{\E[((x'_{ij} - 2 p_i)^2)^2] - \E[(x'_{ij} - 2 p_i)^2)]^2}{(H)^2} \\
    &= \frac{(H) - (H)^2}{(H)^2} \\
    &= \frac{(H)(1  - H)}{(H)^2} \\
    &= \frac{1 - H}{H} \\
    &= \frac{1 - 2 p_i (1 - p_i)}{2 p_i (1 - p_i)} \\
\end{align*}
The full derivation for why $\E[((x'_{ij} - 2 p_i)^2)^2] = \E[(x'_{ij} - 2 p_i)^2] = 2 p_i (1 - p_i)$ is very lengthy algebraically, but can be shortcutted by using the formula for the higher moments of a binomially distributed variable, where $n=2$ and $p=p_i$ \citep{wikipedia2026webBinomial}.
Importantly, the variance of $A_{jj}$ therefore depends on the allele frequencies of the SNPs, even after normalization.
$\bA$ would be a better estimator of the GRM if for the same individual, its variance did not depend on the allele frequency, so we want to adjust $A_{ijj}$ so that $\E[A_{ijj}] = 1$ like before, but also that $\Var[A_{ijj}] = 1$ like for unrelated individuals.

To be frank, I am not sure how the authors derived it, but this equation holds both properties:
$$A_{ijj} = 1 + \frac{{x'}_{ij}^2 - (1 + 2 p_i) x_{ij} + 2 p_i^2}{2 p_i (1 - p_i)} $$
We now explicitly define $\bA$ as follows:
$$
A_{jk} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} A_{ijk} = 
\begin{cases}
\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} \frac{(x'_{ij} - 2 p_i)(x'_{ik} - 2 p_i)}{ 2 p_i (1 - p_i)}, & j \neq k \\[10pt]
1 + \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} \frac{{x'}_{ij}^2 - (1 + 2 p_i) x'_{ij} + 2 p_i^2}{2 p_i (1 - p_i)}, & j = k
\end{cases}
$$

\subsection{Estimating the genetic variance component through REML}
Notice that earlier, we were able to define the phenotype $\by$ as a sum of random effects, treating $\bA$ as a good approximation of $\bG$:
\begin{equation*}
\by = \bg + \bepsilon
\quad\mathrm{where}\quad
\bg \sim \N(\bzero, \varg \bA )
\quad\mathrm{and}\quad
\bepsilon \sim \N(\bzero, \vareps \bI )
\end{equation*}
The unknown parameters here, $\varg$ and $\vareps$, can be estimated through REML (Restricted Maximum Likelihood; since this model doesn't include any fixed effects, simple Maximum Likelihood would also suffice). See the entry on "Maximum Likelihood" for a derivation of how variance component parameters are estimated through REML.

\subsection{Heritability of a polygenic risk score}
Here, we consider a polygenic score (PGS) $\hat{y}_i$ that acts to predict the trait value $y_i$ of an individual.
We assume the PGS only captures signal through the genetic component $g_i$ of the trait and not through the random noise $\epsilon_i$.
That means we can model a polygenic score (PGS) as follows:
$$ \hat{\by} = \bg + \bzeta $$
where $\bzeta \in \R^N$ is random noise in the PGS: $\bzeta \sim \N(\bzero,\sigma^2_\zeta \bI)$.
PGS accuracy is commonly defined by its $\Rsq$ value:
$$ \Rsq = \Corr[\by, \hat{\by}]^2 $$
Under our assumptions, $\Rsq \leq h^2$.
If the phenotype has been standardized so that $\sigma_P^2 = 1$, then $h^2 = \varg$ and $\Rsq \leq \varg$.
We can derive the value of $\sigma^2_\zeta$ as follows:
\begin{align*}
    \Rsq &= \Corr[\by, \hat{\by}]^2 \\
    &= \frac{ \Cov[\by, \hat{\by}]^2 }{ \Var[\by] \Var[\hat{\by}] } \\
    &= \frac{ \Cov[\bg + \bepsilon, \bg + \bzeta]^2 }{ \Var[\bg + \bepsilon] \Var[\bg + \bzeta] }
\end{align*}
Because $\bg$, $\bepsilon$, and $\bzeta$ are independent from each other:
\begin{align*}
    \Cov[\bg + \bepsilon, \bg + \bzeta]
    &= \Cov[\bg, \bg] + \Cov[\bg, \bzeta] + \Cov[\bepsilon, \bg] + \Cov[\bepsilon, \bzeta] \\
    &= \Var[\bg] \\
    &= \varg
\end{align*}
and
\begin{align*}
    \Var[\by] &= \Var[\bg + \bepsilon] = \varg + \vareps = \sigma_P^2 \\
    \Var[\hat{\by}] &= \Var[\bg + \bzeta] = \varg + \sigma_\zeta^2
\end{align*}
Therefore,
\begin{align*}
    \Rsq
    &= \frac{(\varg)^2}{(\varg + \vareps)(\varg + \sigma_\zeta^2)} \\
    &= \frac{(\varg)^2}{\sigma_P^2(\varg + \sigma_\zeta^2)}
\end{align*}
Solving this for $\sigma_\zeta^2$:
\begin{align*}
    \Rsq \sigma_P^2(\varg + \sigma_\zeta^2) &= (\varg)^2 \\
    \varg + \sigma_\zeta^2 &= \frac{(\varg)^2}{\Rsq \sigma_P^2} \\
    \sigma_\zeta^2 &= \frac{(\varg)^2}{\Rsq \sigma_P^2} - \varg \\
    \sigma_\zeta^2 &= \varg \left(\frac{\varg}{\Rsq \sigma_P^2} - 1 \right)
\end{align*}
If we are working with a standardized phenotype, then $\sigma_P^2 = 1$ and this simplifies to:
$$\sigma_\zeta^2 = \varg \left(\frac{\varg}{\Rsq} - 1 \right) $$
This also makes clear why $\Rsq$ cannot be larger than the heritability under this model.
If $\sigma_\zeta^2 = 0$, the PGS is exactly the genetic component, so $\hat{\by} = \bg$ and $\Rsq = h^2$.
Adding PGS noise increases $\Var[\hat{\by}]$ without increasing $\Cov[\by,\hat{\by}]$, so $\Rsq$ decreases.
This also means that:
$$\Var[\hat{\by}] =
\frac{(\varg)^2}{\Rsq \sigma_P^2} =
\frac{\varg h^2}{\Rsq}$$

Suppose that the PGS is then scaled $\hat{\by}' = c\hat{\by}$ such that $\Var[\hat{\by}'] = 1$, where:
$$ c^2 = \frac{1}{\Var[\hat{\by}]} = \frac{\Rsq \sigma_P^2}{(\varg)^2} $$
We can then write the scaled PGS as:
\begin{align*}
    \hat{\by}' &= c(\bg + \bzeta) \\
    &= c\bg + c\bzeta
\end{align*}
The variance explained by the genetic component in the scaled PGS is:
\begin{align*}
    \Var[c\bg]
    &= c^2 \Var[\bg] \\
    &= \frac{\Rsq \sigma_P^2}{(\varg)^2} \varg \\
    &= \frac{\Rsq \sigma_P^2}{\varg} \\
    &= \frac{\Rsq}{h^2}
\end{align*}
and the variance explained by the residual component in the scaled PGS is:
\begin{align*}
    \Var[c\bzeta]
    &= c^2 \Var[\bzeta] \\
    &= \frac{\Rsq \sigma_P^2}{(\varg)^2}
    \left[\frac{(\varg)^2}{\Rsq \sigma_P^2} - \varg\right] \\
    &= 1 - \frac{\Rsq \sigma_P^2}{\varg} \\
    &= 1 - \frac{\Rsq}{h^2}
\end{align*}
Therefore, if we fit the following REML model and treat $\bA$ as a good approximation of the true GRM:
$$ \hat{\by}' \sim \N(\bzero, \sigma^2_1 \bA + \sigma^2_2 \bI) $$
the expected values of $\sigma^2_1$ and $\sigma^2_2$ are:
\begin{align*}
    \E[\sigma^2_1] &= \frac{\Rsq \sigma_P^2}{\varg} = \frac{\Rsq}{h^2} \\
    \E[\sigma^2_2] &= 1 - \frac{\Rsq \sigma_P^2}{\varg} = 1 - \frac{\Rsq}{h^2}
\end{align*}
In other words, the heritability of the scaled PGS is the ratio between PGS accuracy and trait heritability.
