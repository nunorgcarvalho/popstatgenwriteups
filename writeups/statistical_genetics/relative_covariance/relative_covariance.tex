\subsection{Assumptions and approximations}

All formulae and statements follow directly from the text preceding them, which often means establishing assumptions and approximations regarding the relevant variables and processes at play.
Typically, assumptions and approximations are introduced only at the point where they are needed to advance theory further and only in their weakest forms.
Assumptions and approximations hold for all subsequent text except when explicitly withdrawn or replaced later in the document.

An assumption establishes a claim that is treated as exactly true by subsequent text.
In contrast, an approximation takes a claim that is known to be false (either empirically or from theory within the document) but argues that if it is assumed to be true, the error in downstream derivations is negligible.
Equations that follow directly from approximations use the approximation symbol ($\approx$), rather than the equality symbol ($=$).
Approximations are less frequent than assumptions in this document because they typically require empirical knowledge of the typical values of relevant parameters and more advanced derivations in order to assess the expected error.

Both assumptions and approximations often include a ``Related'' section that lists concepts and terminology found in the literature and the document itself that partly or completely overlap with the claim it is making.

\section{Base model of genetic causality and transmission}\label{sec:base-model}

\subsection{Causal form of phenotypes}\label{sec:pheno-form}
Suppose we are studying a population of individuals.
We use $\Yinfo$ to represent all phenotypic information regarding our population, and $y$ denotes a specific \textbf{phenotype} we are studying, centered such that $\E[y] = 0$.
Phenotype $y$ is a function $f_y$ of genetic information $\Ginfo$ and non-genetic (i.e., environmental) information $\Einfo$ of the population, such that for individual $i$, we can write:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pheno-function}
    y_i \;:=\; f_y(\Ginfo_i, \Einfo_i) - \E\big[f_y(\Ginfo_i, \Einfo_i)\big]
\end{equation}
We impose the following constraints on the form of $f_y$ and its components:

\begin{assumption}{Additive-phenotype}{Additive phenotype}
The phenotype is the sum of a \textbf{genetic value} and an \textbf{environmental value},
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:additive-pheno}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:additive-pheno-y}
    y_i \;:=\; g_i + e_i
\end{equation}
where $g$ is a centered function of genetic information only,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:additive-pheno-g}
    g_i \;:=\; f_g(\Ginfo_i) - \E\big[f_g(\Ginfo_i)\big]
\end{equation}
and $e$ is a centered function of environmental information only:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:additive-pheno-e}
    e_i \;:=\; f_e(\Einfo_i) - \E\big[f_e(\Einfo_i)\big]
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}
\related{no gene--environment interaction (GxE).}
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}{GE-indep-within}{Gene--environment independence within individuals}
An individual's environmental value is independent of their own genetic value:
$$ g_i \perp e_i $$
\related{no gene--environment correlation within an individual, no population stratification under strong population structure, implied by \assumptionid{GE-joint-indep-within} (Section~\ref{sec:rm-cov-geno-effects}).}
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}{GE-indep-across}{Gene--environment independence across individuals}
One individual's genetic value is independent of another individual's environmental value:
$$ g_i \perp e_j \ \ (i \neq j) . $$
\related{no indirect (e.g., parental) genetic effects, implied by \assumptionid{GE-joint-indep-across} (Section~\ref{sec:rm-cov-geno-effects}).}
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}{Env-indep}{Independent environments}
The environmental value is independent across individuals, including relatives:
$$ e_i \perp e_j \ \ (i \neq j). $$
\related{no shared environment (e.g., household effects, population stratification), no cultural transmission, no IGEs.}
\end{assumption}

From all possible genetic information about an individual, we focus solely on biallelic genetic variants, called just \textbf{variants} from now on.
For a given variant, each individual possesses two alleles, each inherited from a different parent $u \in \{m,p\}$ (maternal or paternal, respectively) denoted by a superscript.
Later, we also use the index $v \in \{m,p\}$ to denote parent-of-origin.
Each allele is either the reference allele (coded as a $0$) or the alternate allele ($1$).
We can thus define haploid alternate allele counts $G_{ik}^{(m)}, G_{ik}^{(p)} \in \{0,1\}$.
\begin{assumption}{Biallelic}{Biallelic variants only}
The genetic value depends on an individual's genetic information only through their alleles at $M \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ biallelic variants:
$$ f_g(\Ginfo_i) \;=\; f_g\Big(\big\{G_{ik}^{(m)}, G_{ik}^{(p)}\big\}_{k=1}^{M}\Big) . $$
\related{no contribution from multiallelic variants (e.g., structural variants or copy-number variation).}
\end{assumption}

In practice, this assumption is violated as non-biallelic markers (e.g., copy-number variants) causally influence traits.
Later, we will model scenarios where we have only genotyped a subset of biallelic variants and derive quantities that depend on genotyped biallelic variants vs all genetic variants.
We will make the approximation that the contribution of non-genotyped non-biallelic variants is similar in form to that of non-genotyped biallelic variants. 

Each variant's effect on the genetic value is additive across $M$ variants:
\begin{assumption}{Additivity}{Additive genetic value}
    An individual's genetic value is the sum of each variant's effect, $f_g^{(k)}$:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:additivity}
        f_g(\Ginfo_i) \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{M} f_g^{(k)}(G_{ik}^{(m)},G_{ik}^{(p)})
    \end{equation}
    \related{no gene-gene interactions (e.g., epistasis).}
\end{assumption}

Summing haploid alternate allele counts yields an individual's \textbf{genotype} at a variant $G_{ik} \in \{0,1,2\}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:genotype}
    G_{ik} \;:=\; G_{ik}^{(m)} + G_{ik}^{(p)}
\end{equation}
We assume that a variant's effect can be described by a linear function whose slope is the variant's causal \textbf{effect size} $\beta_k \in \R$:

\begin{assumption}{Linearity}{Linear variant effects}
Each variant's contribution to the genetic value is a linear function of the individual's genotype at that variant:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:linearity}
    f_g^{(k)}\big(G_{ik}^{(m)}, G_{ik}^{(p)}\big) \;=\; \beta_k\, G_{ik} .
\end{equation}
\related{no dominance, no parent-of-origin effects, no cis-vs-trans GxG distinction.}
\end{assumption}

A variant is causal if and only if $\beta_k \neq 0$.
Because we assume a linear and additive model, the genetic value can also be called a \emph{breeding value}, although we stick to the former term.

\subsection{Variables}

\subsubsection{Centering and standardization}\label{sec:standardization}
We define the \textbf{allele frequency} of a variant $p_k \in (0,1)$ to be the expected allele of an individual:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:allele-freq}
    p_k \;:=\; \E[G_{ik}^{(u)}] \qquad \text{for } u \in \{m, p\}
\end{equation}
It follows that the expected genotype of a variant is twice its allele frequency, $\E[G_{ik}] = 2 p_k$.
It also follows that $\Var[G_{ik}^{(u)}] = p_k (1 - p_k)$, an expression so ubiquitous throughout this document that we introduce the shorthand $q_k := 1 - p_k$ so that we can write $\Var[G_{ik}^{(u)}] = p_k q_k$.
From now on, we only work with \emph{centered} versions of alleles, $x_{ik}^{(u)} \in (-1,1)$, and genotypes, $x_{ik} \in (-2,2)$, defined as:
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:centered}
\begin{align}
    x_{ik}^{(u)} \;&:=\; G_{ik}^{(u)} - p_k \label{eq:centered-allele} \\
    x_{ik} \;&:=\; x_{ik}^{(m)} + x_{ik}^{(p)} \;=\; G_{ik} - 2 p_k \label{eq:centered-genotype}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
This results in $\E[x_{ik}^{(u)}] = \E[x_{ik}] = 0$ and an unchanged variance, $\Var[x_{ik}^{(u)}] = p_k q_k$.
Note that it does not follow that $\Var[x_{ik}] = 2 p_k q_k$, which requires $\Cov[x_{ik}^{(m)}, x_{ik}^{(p)}] = 0$, something we have not yet shown.
We later demonstrate (Equation~\eqref{eq:var-x}) that this is approximately the case under random mating, motivating the \emph{standardized} versions of alleles and genotypes, $\tilde{x}_{ik}^{(u)}, \tilde{x}_{ik} \in \R$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:standardized}
    \tilde{x}_{ik}^{(u)} \;:=\; \frac{x_{ik}^{(u)}}{\sqrt{2 p_k q_k}} , \qquad
    \tilde{x}_{ik} \;:=\; \frac{x_{ik}}{\sqrt{2 p_k q_k}}
\end{equation}

Standardized alleles always have a variance of $\Var[\tilde{x}_{ik}^{(u)}] = \tfrac12$.
We also define \emph{standardized effect sizes}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:standardized-effect}
    \tilde{\beta}_k \;:=\; \beta_k \sqrt{2 p_k q_k}
\end{equation}

 Under \assumptionid{Linearity}, the genetic value is the linear combination of genotypes with effect size coefficients, using either the standardized or unstandardized versions of both:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:genetic-value}
\begin{aligned}
    g_i \;=\; f_g(\Ginfo_i) - \E\big[f_g(\Ginfo_i)\big]
    \;&=\; \sum_{k=1}^{M} \beta_k \big(G_{ik} - 2p_k\big) \\
    \;&=\; \sum_{k=1}^{M} \beta_k\, x_{ik} \\
    \;&=\; \sum_{k=1}^{M} \tilde{\beta}_k\, \tilde{x}_{ik}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Effect sizes and functional information}\label{sec:effect-sizes}
A main goal of statistical genetics is to quantify the effect size of variants, $\bbeta$.
Here, we propose a model whereby effect sizes are determined by their biological properties alone, meaning that individual effect sizes are fixed, not random variables drawn from some distribution.

A slightly-philosophical complication is that effect sizes are clearly always dependent on the environment in some sense.
A trivial but outlandish example is suppose that there is some variant that influences the expression of some gene, which then influences downstream traits.
For some reason, the population's society injects every newborn with a molecule that completely blocks the expression of that gene.
In this society, the variant ceases to have an effect on the gene's downstream traits.
So, a truly principled discussion of ``biological'' effects requires grounding out the environment that the population exists in, which we encode in $\mathbf{e}$.
We will make the following implicit assumption explicit:

\begin{assumption}{Effects-env-constant}{Effect sizes are constant across environment}
The effect size of a variant, $\beta_k$, is constant across all environmental values, $e_i$, in a population.
We do not impose this constraint on environments not found in the population.
\related{no gene--environment interaction (GxE).}
\end{assumption}

We define a variant $k$'s \emph{functional information}, $\Binfo_k$, to be the biological and trait-relevant properties of the variant that inform its effect size.
For example, variants in coding regions tend to be more biologically impactful than variants in non-coding regions, so this functional property can causally influence effect sizes.

We model a variant $k$'s effect size as being a deterministic and trait-specific function, $\fbeta$, of its functional information, $\Binfo_k$, and a sign indicator $\delta_k \in \{-1, 1\}$ that depends on how its alleles are encoded:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:effect-size-function}
    \beta_k \;:=\; \delta_k \sqrt{\fbeta(\Binfo_k)}
\end{equation}

Note that our usage of $y$ as a superscript for $f_\beta$ conveys that the mapping of functional information (which is trait-invariant) onto effect sizes is trait-specific.
For notational consistency, we should also include such a superscript when writing out $\beta_k$, but we avoid doing so for the sake of notational clarity.
We assume that alleles are assigned randomly such that:

\begin{assumption}{Effects-first-moment-zero}{Effect sizes are mean-zero}
The expected effect size of each variant is zero:
$$ \E[\beta_k] \;=\; 0 . $$
This follows from assuming that trait-increasing alleles are randomly chosen to be the alternate vs reference allele, thus resulting in $\E[\delta_k]=0$, which is independent of $\Binfo_k$.
\related{the infinitesimal / random-effects model, symmetric effects.}
\end{assumption}

It then follows that:
$$ \beta^2_k \;=\; \fbeta(\Binfo_k) $$

We note here that while effect sizes and functional properties are fixed, they will often be treated as if they are random variables.
The randomness we're modeling is driven by our uncertainty in distinguishing individual variants.
Conditioning on $\bbeta$ simply means the effect sizes are known, while not conditioning on $\bbeta$ is akin to averaging across all variants.
We can also condition on other variant-specific information, like allele frequency $p_k$ or individual genotypes $\mathbf{G}_{k}$, thus averaging across variants that share that information.

\subsubsection{Variance components}
The \textbf{additive (genetic) variance} of a trait, $V_A \in \R_{\geq 0}$, is the variance of the genetic value $g_i$ of a random individual $i$ from the population.
It is conditioned on the genotypic information of the population, $\Ginfo$, as well known effect sizes, $\bbeta$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:additive-variance}
    V_A \;:=\; \Var\big[g_i \mid \Ginfo, \bbeta\big]
\end{equation}
Similarly, the \textbf{environmental variance} of the trait, $V_E \in \R_{\geq 0}$, is the variance of the environmental value:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:environmental-variance}
    V_E \;:=\; \Var[e_i \mid \Einfo]
\end{equation}
Finally, the \textbf{phenotypic variance} of the trait, $V_Y \in \R_{\geq 0}$, is the variance of the phenotypic value itself:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phenotypic-variance}
    V_Y \;:=\; \Var[y_i \mid \Ginfo, \bbeta, \Einfo]
\end{equation}
We define the \textbf{heritability} of the trait, $h^2 \in [0,1]$, to be:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heritability}
    h^2 \;:=\; \frac{V_A}{V_Y}
\end{equation}
It is common practice to also standardize the phenotype such that $\Var[y] = 1$, which leads to $h^2 = V_A$.
However, our derivations will not assume that phenotypes are standardized, only centered.

Different kinds of heritabilities exist.
Many are primarily a reflection of the estimator used, like SNP heritability and GWAS heritability, while others are better understood as different types of estimands, like broad-sense and narrow-sense heritability.
Given our trait architecture assumptions (\assumptionid{Additivity} and \assumptionid{Linearity}), the formula above best reflects \textbf{narrow-sense heritability}, which is the proportion of trait variance attributable to additive genetic effects.
Other kinds of heritabilities will be discussed when relevant.

\subsection{Pedigrees and paths}\label{sec:pedpath}
So far, we have discussed four categories of information: phenotypic ($\Yinfo$), genetic ($\Ginfo$), environmental ($\Einfo$), and biological ($\Binfo$).
A fifth category of information is the \textbf{pedigree}, $\Pinfo$, which describes familial relations between individuals in a population.
A pedigree is a graph composed of nodes and edges, where each node represents an individual.
A major goal of ours is to condense the information in a full pedigree down to sets of paths between pairs of individuals.
To understand which paths are useful to us, we need to establish the different types of edges that connect nodes in the pedigree.

\subsubsection{Types of edges in a pedigree}

\paragraph{Mating}
Individuals in a population may form pairs, such as to produce offspring, which we call \textbf{mate} pairs.
Note that if the pair-formation process is the same for non-offspring-producing pairs (e.g., homosexual couples), then we still model and refer to them as mates.
We do not make any assumptions about monogamy, i.e., an individual can be part of multiple mate pairs.
Mating pairs are represented by an undirected edge between them in the pedigree.
The pedigree constrained to just nodes and mating edges is called a \emph{mating graph}.

\paragraph{Meiosis}
While the term \textbf{meiosis} specifically refers to the biological process of gamete-formation, here we use it to describe the transmission of alleles from parent to offspring.
Understanding meiosis does not require knowledge of the offspring's other parent, but it is true nonetheless that meiotic processes always occur in pairs from both mating parents.
Meiotic transmission is represented by a directed edge in the pedigree pointing from parent to offspring.
The pedigree constrained to just nodes and meiotic edges is called a \emph{meiosis graph}.

\paragraph{Other building blocks}
It is possible to come up with any other type of edge that connects nodes and constrains the joint distribution of information across individuals.
For example, adopted children do not follow the same laws of meiosis as biological offspring, but may still be influenced by non-genetic transmission shared with biological offspring.
We only introduce these additional types of edges when needed.

All subsequent analysis of pedigrees is greatly simplified with the following assumption:

\begin{assumption}{Discrete-generations}{Discrete, non-overlapping generations}
The population is partitioned into generations indexed by $t \in \mathbb{Z}$, and every individual mates within their own generation.
Offspring produced from such a mating are of generation $t+1$.
\related{no overlapping generations, no mating across generations, sequential generations between parent-offspring pairs.}
\end{assumption}

\subsubsection{Mating chains}
A \emph{run} of edges is a sequence of edges in which each edge begins at the node where the previous one ends.
In order to describe the mating graph in terms of runs, we'll need the following assumption:

\begin{assumption}{No-mating-loops}{The mating graph is a forest}
    A \emph{cycle} in the mating graph is a run of edges that returns to its starting node without visiting any other node twice.
    A graph with no cycles is a \emph{forest}, and each of its connected components is a \emph{tree}.

    We assume the mating graph is a forest.
    As a consequence, two nodes in the same tree are joined by exactly one run of edges that visits no node twice, and two nodes in different trees are joined by none.
\end{assumption}

For $i \neq j$ in the same tree, the \emph{mating chain} $\Mu(i,j)$ is that unique run of edges connecting them, read as the ordered list of individuals it passes through, from $i$ to $j$.
The \emph{mating distance} $\dmu_{ij} \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \cup \{\infty\}$ is the number of edges on that chain, i.e., the number of matings crossed in getting from $i$ to $j$, and is one fewer than the size of it:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mating-dist}
    \dmu_{ij} \;:=\; \big|\Mu(i,j)\big| - 1
\end{equation}
We additionally set $\dmu_{ii} = 0$, and $\dmu_{ij} = \infty$ when $i$ and $j$ lie in different trees, meaning no sequence of matings connects them at all.

\subsubsection{Lineal chains}\label{sec:lineal-chains}
The meiosis graph consists only of the directed edges (parent-to-offspring meioses) between nodes.
Every individual has two edges coming in, one from each parent.
A \emph{directed run} is a sequence of alternating nodes and edges that always follows the direction of the edges.
A directed run can be traced from a node onto itself (i.e., is zero-length).
Under \assumptionid{Discrete-generations} every edge steps from one generation to the next, so following edges forward always moves forward in time.
Therefore, the meiosis graph is guaranteed to be a \emph{directed acyclic graph}.

Let $t_i \in \mathbb{Z}$ denote the generation that an individual $i$ belongs to, according to \assumptionid{Discrete-generations}.
Let $m(i)$ and $p(i)$ denote the two parents, mother and father, of an individual $i$, i.e., the two nodes with an edge into $i$.
If a directed run leads from $j$ to $i$, then $j$ is an \textbf{ancestor} of $i$, $i$ is a \textbf{descendant} of $j$, and the two are \textbf{lineal relatives} to each other.

For an ancestor $j$ of $i$, the \emph{lineal chain} $\Lin(j,i)$ is a directed run from $j$ (the source) to $i$ (the sink), read as the ordered list of individuals it passes through.
The \emph{lineal distance} $\dlin_{ij} \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} \cup \{\infty\}$ is the number of edges on that chain, i.e., the number of meioses separating the two, and is symmetric, $\dlin_{ij} = \dlin_{ji}$.
Under \assumptionid{Discrete-generations}, it is just the number of generations between them:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lineal-dist}
    \dlin_{ij} \;:=\; \big|\Lin(j,i)\big| - 1 \;=\; \big|\, t_i - t_j \,\big|
\end{equation}
We additionally set $\dlin_{ii} = 0$, and $\dlin_{ij} = \infty$ if $i$ and $j$ are not lineal relatives.

\subsubsection{Pedigree paths}\label{sec:pedigree-paths}

A \emph{pedigree path} $\PedPath(i,j)$ between \textbf{probands} $i$ and $j$ is a sequence of chains, alternating between lineal and mating, in which each chain shares an end individual with the next.
The first chain has an end at $i$, the last has an end at $j$, and no individual is visited twice across the entire pedigree path.
Note that lineal chains in a pedigree path can be traversed in either direction (up or down the chain).
The double-headed arrow marks that the two ends play symmetric roles: $\PedPath(j,i)$ is $\PedPath(i,j)$ read backwards (the same holds for mating chains).
We can think of a pedigree path itself as being a \emph{chain digraph}, where the nodes are chains, and the edges connect lineal chains to mating chains, or vice-versa.
Since mating chains contain individuals of the same generation while lineal chains contain individuals spanning multiple generations, we enforce edges to be directed such that the older chain points to the younger chain.
The number of edges leaving a chain node is its out-degree, $N_o \in \{0,1,2\}$, and the number of edges entering it is its in-degree.
Non-terminal lineal chains will always have an out-degree of 1 and an in-degree of 1.

Furthermore, we require that whenever a mating chain has an out-edge, the mating chain must contain both the parents of the second-oldest individual in the lineal chain it points to.
In other words, the terminal individual node of two connected lineal and mating chains must be the ``outer'' parent.

\paragraph{Dominant path} In a full pedigree, any two individuals will have at least one pedigree path connecting them, since everyone shares a common ancestor.
In fact, arbitrarily many pedigree paths can be drawn, since all ancestors across individuals also share their own common ancestors.
The \emph{dominant path} is the single best pedigree path at condensing the information of the pedigree regarding two individuals.
The exact meaning of ``best'' depends on the specific applications discussed later, but can be broadly thought as the pedigree path that most closely approximates the statistical consequences of the full pedigree.

\subsection{Transmission}\label{sec:transmission}
A sixth category of information is transmission, $\Tinfo$, which catalogs which parental alleles were inherited by each individual in the population.
Unless we are discussing time-dependent processes (like assortative mating not under equilibrium), we omit the notational use of $t$ for clarity.
We make the following explicit assumption about the dynamics of allele frequencies:
\begin{assumption}{Constant-freq}{Constant allele frequencies}
The allele frequency $p_k$ of each variant is the same in every generation:
$$ \E\big[G_{ik}^{(u)}\big] \;=\; p_k \qquad \text{for every individual } i \text{ of every generation} . $$
\related{no selection, no mutations, no migration, no drift (i.e., infinite population size).}
\end{assumption}

\subsubsection{Alleles}\label{sec:transmission-alleles}
While already implicitly covered in \assumptionid{Constant-freq}, we make the following claim explicit, but frame it as an \emph{approximation}:

\begin{approximation}{No-mutation}{No mutations}
Every transmitted allele is an exact copy of the parental allele it was inherited from; 
i.e., no de novo mutations occur.
\justification{Empirically, mutation rates are very low: on the order of $10^{-8}$ per base pair per generation in humans.}
\end{approximation}

An offspring's allele is a function of the transmitting parent's two alleles.

\begin{assumption}{Segregation}{Segregation}
    Individual $i$'s allele inherited from parent $u(i)$ is one of the two alleles of $u(i)$:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:segregation}
        x^{(u)}_{ik} \;:=\; T^{(u)}_{ik}\, x^{(m)}_{u(i),k} \;+\; \big(1 - T^{(u)}_{ik}\big)\, x^{(p)}_{u(i),k}
    \end{equation}
    where $T^{(u)}_{ik} \in \{0,1\}$ is an indicator variable for whether the maternally-inherited ($1$) or paternally-inherited ($0$) allele of parent $u(i)$ was transmitted to $i$.
    \related{Mendel's law of segregation.}
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}{Independent-assortment}{Independent assortment}
    Every transmission indicator is an independent uniform Bernoulli trial:
    $$ T^{(u)}_{ik} \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\tfrac12), $$
    independently across variants $k$, parents $u$ and offspring $i$, and independently of every allele except those it passes on (the offspring's, and their descendants').
    \related{Mendel's law of independent assortment, no physical linkage (every pair of variants recombines with probability $\tfrac12$), independent meioses across offspring, no meiotic drive.}
\end{assumption}

It follows that the expected offspring allele given the transmitting parent's alleles is given by:
$$ \E[x^{(u)}_{ik} \mid x^{(m)}_{u(i),k}, x^{(p)}_{u(i),k}] \;=\;
\tfrac12 (x^{(m)}_{u(i),k} + x^{(p)}_{u(i),k}) \;=\; 
\tfrac12 x_{u(i),k} \;=\; 
\E[x^{(u)}_{ik} \mid x_{u(i),k} ] $$
We can alternatively express the offspring allele as the sum of its expected value plus an uncorrelated segregation residual:
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:allele-transmit}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:allele-transmit-resid}
    x^{(u)}_{ik} \;=\; \tfrac12 x_{u(i),k} + x^{\perp(u)}_{ik}
\end{equation}
Note that our use $\perp$ here is misleading because while the residual is uncorrelated with the parental genotype, it is not independent of it.
An alternative parameterization of the segregation residual is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:allele-transmit-T}
    x^{\perp(u)}_{ik} \;=\; \big(T^{(u)}_{ik} - \tfrac12\big)\big(x^{(m)}_{u(i),k} - x^{(p)}_{u(i),k}\big)
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}

\subsubsection{Genotypes}
We can express genotype transmission the same way:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:genotype-transmit}
\begin{aligned}
    x_{ik} \;&=\; x^{(m)}_{ik} + x^{(p)}_{ik} \\
    \;&=\; \tfrac12 x_{m(i),k} + x^{\perp(m)}_{ik} \;+\; \tfrac12 x_{p(i),k} + x^{\perp(p)}_{ik} \\
    \;&=\; \tfrac12 x_{m(i),k} + \tfrac12 x_{p(i),k} + x^{\perp}_{ik}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $x^{\perp}_{ik} \;:=\; x^{\perp(m)}_{ik} + x^{\perp(p)}_{ik}$.
Each allele residual has mean zero given its parent's alleles, so the genotype residual has mean zero given both parents' alleles, and
$$ \E\big[x_{ik} \mid x_{m(i),k},\, x_{p(i),k}\big] \;=\; \tfrac12 x_{m(i),k} + \tfrac12 x_{p(i),k} . $$

\subsubsection{Genetic values}
Finally, we can also express genetic value transmission:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:g-transmit}
\begin{aligned}
    g_i \;&=\; \sum_{k=1}^{M} \beta_k\, x_{ik} \\
    \;&=\; \sum_{k=1}^{M} \beta_k \Big(\tfrac12 x_{m(i),k} + \tfrac12 x_{p(i),k} + x^{\perp}_{ik}\Big) \\
    \;&=\; \tfrac12 g_{m(i)} + \tfrac12 g_{p(i)} + g^{\perp}_i ,
    \qquad g^{\perp}_i \;:=\; \sum_{k=1}^{M} \beta_k\, x^{\perp}_{ik} .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The residual $g^{\perp}_i$ has mean zero given the parents' alleles, and $g_{m(i)}$ and $g_{p(i)}$ are functions of those alleles, so
$$ \E\big[g_i \mid g_{m(i)},\, g_{p(i)}\big] \;=\; \tfrac12 g_{m(i)} + \tfrac12 g_{p(i)} . $$

\subsection{Identity-by-state (IBS)}\label{sec:ibs}

Two alleles of the same variant are said to be \textbf{identical-by-state (IBS)} if they have the same allelic state:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ibs}
\begin{aligned}
    \IBS\big(G^{(u)}_{ik}, G^{(v)}_{jk}\big)
    \;&:=\; \begin{cases}
        1 & G^{(u)}_{ik} = G^{(v)}_{jk} \\
        0 & G^{(u)}_{ik} \neq G^{(v)}_{jk}
    \end{cases} \\[4pt]
    \;&=\; 1 - \big(G^{(u)}_{ik} - G^{(v)}_{jk}\big)^2
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The second step follows from each allele count being either $0$ or $1$, so that their squared difference is $1$ exactly when the two differ and $0$ when they are equal.
The average IBS value across all same-variant allele pairs between two individuals is their \textbf{IBS proportion}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ibs-proportion}
    \overline{\IBS}_{ij} \;:=\; \frac{1}{4M} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \sum_{u,v \in \{m,p\}} \IBS\big(G^{(u)}_{ik}, G^{(v)}_{jk}\big)
\end{equation}

Cross-products have clean statistical properties and genotype data is often not phased.
Therefore, we define \emph{genotypic similarity}, $A_{ij} \in \R$, to be an IBS-based measure of relatedness equivalent to the average \emph{standardized} genotype cross-product across all same-variant pairs:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grm}
    A_{ij} \;:=\; \frac{1}{M} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \tilde{x}_{ik} \tilde{x}_{jk}
\end{equation}
With unstandardized genotypes (Equation~\eqref{eq:centered}), this is $A_{ij} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \frac{x_{ik} x_{jk}}{2 p_k q_k}$.
Note that since we define $A_{ij}$ in terms of standardized genotypes, rarer variants contribute relatively more, a choice motivated by the distribution of effect sizes, discussed more in Section~\ref{sec:effect-size-dist}.
Genotypic similarity can be parameterized in terms of allele cross-products by expanding each genotype into its two alleles:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grm-alleles}
\begin{aligned}
    A_{ij} \;&=\; \frac{1}{M} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \big(\tilde{x}^{(m)}_{ik} + \tilde{x}^{(p)}_{ik}\big)\big(\tilde{x}^{(m)}_{jk} + \tilde{x}^{(p)}_{jk}\big) \\[4pt]
    \;&=\; \frac{1}{M} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \sum_{u,v \in \{m,p\}} \tilde{x}^{(u)}_{ik}\, \tilde{x}^{(v)}_{jk}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The matrix $\bA$ defined for all $i,j$ pairs is called the \textbf{genetic relationship matrix (GRM)}.
Observe that IBS-based measures of relatedness only depend on genetic information $\Ginfo$ in the form of allelic or genotypic states.
Often, not all relevant variants are genotyped.
Suppose we order variants such that $k \in \{1 , ... , M_T\}$ index all the genotyped variants, where $M_T \leq M$, while the remaining variants are ungenotyped.
We can consider genotypic similarity computed from genotyped variants to be an estimate of the true genotypic similarity:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grm-hat}
    \widehat{A}_{ij} \;:=\; \frac{1}{M_T} \sum_{k=1}^{M_T} \tilde{x}_{ik} \tilde{x}_{jk}
\end{equation}

Later, we will explore how the two differ in their relationship to useful estimands.

\subsection{Identity-by-descent (IBD)}\label{sec:ibd}

\subsubsection{Coalescence}\label{sec:coalescence}

In Section~\ref{sec:lineal-chains}, parent-offspring pairs define the meiosis graph.
For each variant, the \emph{coalescence graph} is the allele-level version of the meiosis graph, where each allele is a node and directed edges represent parent-to-offspring transmission.
Each individual has two alleles and is therefore represented by two nodes in a coalescence graph, and like a meiosis graph, a coalescence graph is a directed acyclic graph.

Similarly, a \emph{transmission chain} is the allele-level version of a lineal chain: it is a directed run in the coalescence graph, starting from an ancestral allele (the source) and ending at a descendant allele (the sink).
We write $\Tchain^{(t)}(a)$ the denote the unique transmission chain whose sink is allele $a$ and whose source is $a$'s ancestral allele in generation $t$, where $t \leq t_a$.
Note that this allows for transmission chains with zero-length if $t = t_a$.
Section~\ref{sec:ibd-pedigree} develops transmission chains in more detail.

Consider two alleles $a$ and $b$ of the same variant.
A \emph{coalescence path} $\Cpath(a,b)$ is a pair of transmission chains, one with sink $a$ and one with sink $b$, that have the same source and share no other allele (i.e., it is the most recent allele that can act as a source to both $a$ and $b$).
When one allele is itself the ancestor of the other, e.g., a parent's allele and the copy their offspring inherited, then one of the two chains will have length zero.
The common source is the \emph{coalescence point} of $a$ and $b$, and the length of the path, $|\Cpath(a,b)|$, is the total number of meioses on its two chains.
The coalescence point is unique and guaranteed to exist in a finite population, since all individuals share a common ancestor.
Appendix~\ref{app:vocabulary} aggregates the different types of graphs, paths, and chains discussed throughout this document.

A contradiction emerges from our assumptions and approximations so far.
Specifically, only two of the following statements can be true at once: (1) all alleles eventually coalesce, (2) variation in allelic state exists, and (3) mutations do not occur.
Yet, the first claim is true, as just explained.
The second claim is true because we restrict our analyses to \textbf{polymorphic} sites (implied by $p_k \in (0,1)$ in Section~\ref{sec:standardization}).
And the third claim is true if we take the \approximationid{No-mutation} approximation to be exactly true.

Clearly, the easiest way to resolve this is to admit that \approximationid{No-mutation} is not true, something we already know from biology.
Allowing for mutations resolves the contradiction because two alleles with different states are the result of a mutation occurring at some point in their coalescence path.

However, modeling mutations complicates analysis with little gain when studying small time frames.
So instead, we work with the \emph{recent coalescence graph}: the coalescence graph restricted to alleles that are not older than some \emph{baseline generation}, $t_B \in \mathbb{Z}$ (i.e., $t \geq t_B$).
As a consequence, two alleles are no longer guaranteed to have a \emph{recent coalescence path}, since they can coalesce prior to the baseline generation.
This lets us replace \approximationid{No-mutation} with a much weaker form that resolves the contradiction, ensures the previous derivations still hold, and leads to practically useful measures:

\begin{assumption}{No-recent-mutation}{No mutations since the baseline generation}
Every transmission into a generation $t \geq t_B$ copies the parental allele exactly.
I.e., no de novo mutation occurs on any path of the recent coalescence graph.
\related{recent IBD implies IBS.}
\end{assumption}

Two alleles are said to be \textbf{identical-by-descent (IBD)} if they \emph{recently coalesce}.
Non-IBS allele pairs are guaranteed to be non-IBD, and, equivalently, IBD pairs are necessarily IBS.
Alleles are considered to be IBD with themselves.
This notion of IBD implicitly makes the following assumption, which we explicitly restate:

\begin{assumption}{Outbred-founders}{Founder alleles are not IBD with each other}
No two distinct alleles of the baseline generation $t_B$ (called founder alleles) are IBD, whether they belong to the same individual or to different individuals.
\related{non-inbred founders; founders unrelated to each other; together with \assumptionid{Unrelated-mates} (Section~\ref{sec:ibd-well-behaved}), makes a pedigree outbred.}
\end{assumption}

\subsubsection{Measures of relatedness}\label{sec:measures-relatedness}

We can use genome-wide IBD state to construct measures of relatedness.
IBD is a form of transmission information $\Tinfo$ because it constrains the distribution of the transmission indicators $T$ across the pedigree to guarantee the existence of the coalescence path.

The \textbf{coefficient of kinship}, $\Theta_{ij} \in [0,1]$, between two individuals is the proportion of allele pairs between the two that are IBD.
For notational ease, we also define an allele-specific version of the coefficient of kinship, $\Theta^{(uv)}_{ijk} \in \{0,1\}$, which is just the IBD state of two alleles:
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:kinship-all}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:allele-kinship-def}
    \Theta^{(uv)}_{ijk} \;:=\; \IBD(G^{(u)}_{ik},G^{(v)}_{jk})
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kinship}
    \Theta_{ij}
    \;:=\; \frac{1}{4M} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \sum_{u,v \in \{m,p\}} \IBD(G^{(u)}_{ik},G^{(v)}_{jk})
    \;=\; \frac{1}{4M} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \sum_{u,v \in \{m,p\}} \Theta^{(uv)}_{ijk}
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}

The \textbf{coefficient of relatedness}, $\pi_{ij} \in [0,2]$, is simply twice the coefficient of kinship:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:relatedness}
    \pi_{ij} \;:=\; 2\Theta_{ij}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Inbreeding} is when a genotype's two alleles recently coalesce, implying that the parents of the proband have a \emph{recent common ancestor} (not younger than $t\geq t_B$).
The \textbf{coefficient of inbreeding} of an individual, $f_i \in [0,1]$, is the excess coefficient of relatedness they have with themselves:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inbreeding}
\begin{aligned}
    f_i \;&:=\; \pi_{ii} - 1 \;=\; 2\Theta_{ii} - 1 \\[4pt]
    \;&=\; \frac{2}{4M} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \Big[ \Theta^{(mm)}_{iik} + \Theta^{(pp)}_{iik} + 2\,\Theta^{(mp)}_{iik} \Big] - 1 \\[4pt]
    \;&=\; \frac{1}{2M} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \Big[ 2 + 2\,\Theta^{(mp)}_{iik} \Big] - 1 \\[4pt]
    \;&=\; \frac{1}{M} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \Theta^{(mp)}_{iik}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
We can also define the variant-specific version of the inbreeding coefficient to be the IBD state of an individual genotype's two alleles, $f_{ik} := \Theta^{(mp)}_{iik} \in \{0,1\}$.
It follows that the coefficient of inbreeding is the genome-wide average of its variant-specific coefficient, $f_i = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{k=1}^{M} f_{ik}$.

The term \textbf{outbred}, used earlier in \assumptionid{Outbred-founders}, refers to lack of inbreeding and can be applied at different levels.
A genotype is outbred if its two alleles are not IBD, an individual is outbred if all of its genotypes are outbred, and a pedigree is outbred if all of its individuals are outbred.

\subsubsection{Transmission of IBD states}\label{sec:ibd-transmission}
Our next goal is to establish a framework for deriving the expected coefficient of relatedness between two individuals given their relationship in a pedigree.
As described in Section~\ref{sec:transmission}, parents pass on alleles to their offspring, who then pass it onto their offspring, and so on.
Therefore, IBD properties for a single generation of transmission are the building blocks for generalizing to other relatives.

\textbf{Path analysis} is a powerful tool for determining the covariance between variables given a causal model, represented by a directed graph called a \textbf{path diagram}.
Appendix~\ref{app:path-analysis} contains an overview of how path analysis works and how path diagrams visualize relationships between variables.
To make IBD compatible with the path analysis framework, we define \textbf{founder alleles}, $\mathring{x}$, to be the alleles possessed by the baseline generation (i.e., \textbf{founders}) that are transmitted down the pedigree.
Subsequently, $\mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)}$ references the specific founder allele that individual $i$'s $u$-sided allele at variant $k$ is ultimately inherited from.
To establish the equivalence between probability (IBD) and correlation (path analysis), we use centered alleles, $x$, and treat each founder allele as having its own independent effect that is completely uncorrelated with every other founder allele (\assumptionid{Outbred-founders}).
Alleles inherited from founder alleles keep their statistical properties.
Note that transmission information requires the scaffold of a pedigree to determine whose alleles can ultimately be passed onto whom, so conditioning on $\Tinfo$ already implicitly conditions on $\Pinfo$.

Continuing from Section~\ref{sec:transmission-alleles}, the structural equation for an individual's allele in terms of founder alleles is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:founder-transmit}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathring{x}^{(u)}_{ik}
    \;&=\; T^{(u)}_{ik}\, \mathring{x}^{(m)}_{u(i),k} \;+\; \big(1 - T^{(u)}_{ik}\big)\, \mathring{x}^{(p)}_{u(i),k} \\
    \;&=\; \tfrac12 \mathring{x}^{(m)}_{u(i),k} + \tfrac12 \mathring{x}^{(p)}_{u(i),k} + \mathring{x}^{\perp(u)}_{ik}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\mathring{x}^{\perp(u)}_{ik}$, the founder-allele version of Equation~\eqref{eq:allele-transmit-T}, is an uncorrelated residual such that the variance of each allele in the graph is fixed to $p_k q_k$, as required by Section~\ref{sec:standardization}.
IBD state and implied correlation between two alleles is therefore tracked based on whether the alleles they inherit from are identical:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ibd-founder}
\begin{aligned}
    \Theta^{(uv)}_{ijk} \;=\;
    \IBD\big(x_{ik}^{(u)}, x_{jk}^{(v)}\big)
    \;&=\; \begin{cases}
        1 & \mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)} = \mathring{x}_{jk}^{(v)} \\
        0 & \mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)} \neq \mathring{x}_{jk}^{(v)}
    \end{cases} \\
    \;&=\; \frac{\Cov\big[\mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)}, \mathring{x}_{jk}^{(v)} \;\big|\; \Tinfo\big]}{p_k q_k} \\
    \;&=\; \Corr\Big[\mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)}, \mathring{x}_{jk}^{(v)} \;\Big|\; \Tinfo \Big]
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Expected IBD within a nuclear family}

When we don't have transmission information, $\Tinfo$, we can still derive the expected IBD state given the pedigree alone, $\Pinfo$.
Since the pedigree is not variant-specific, its conditional expectations written below will omit the variant index $k$.
We treat this expected IBD state as an estimate of the realized variant-specific IBD state.
Converting from conditioning on transmission information to conditioning on pedigree information requires the law of total covariance.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:allele-kinship}
\begin{aligned}
    \widehat\Theta^{(uv)}_{ij}
    \;&:=\; \E\big[\Theta^{(uv)}_{ijk} \;\big|\; \Pinfo\big] \\
    \;&=\; \E\Big[\, \Corr\big[\mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)}, \mathring{x}_{jk}^{(v)} \;\big|\; \Tinfo\big] \;\Big|\; \Pinfo \Big] \\
    \;&=\; \frac{1}{p_k q_k}\, \E\Big[\, \Cov\big[\mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)}, \mathring{x}_{jk}^{(v)} \;\big|\; \Tinfo\big] \;\Big|\; \Pinfo \Big] \\
    \;&=\; \frac{1}{p_k q_k} \Big( \Cov\big[\mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)}, \mathring{x}_{jk}^{(v)} \;\big|\; \Pinfo\big] \\
    &\qquad\qquad\quad -\; \Cov\Big[\, \E\big[\mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)} \mid \Tinfo\big],\, \E\big[\mathring{x}_{jk}^{(v)} \mid \Tinfo\big] \;\Big|\; \Pinfo \Big] \Big) \\
    \;&=\; \frac{\Cov\big[\mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)}, \mathring{x}_{jk}^{(v)} \;\big|\; \Pinfo\big]}{\sqrt{\Var\big[\mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)} \;\big|\; \Pinfo\big]\, \Var\big[\mathring{x}_{jk}^{(v)} \;\big|\; \Pinfo\big]}} \\
    \;&=\; \Corr\big[\mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)}, \mathring{x}_{jk}^{(v)} \;\big|\; \Pinfo\big] .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The second line substitutes Equation~\eqref{eq:ibd-founder}, and the third uses $\Var[\mathring{x}^{(u)}_{ik} \mid \Tinfo] = p_k q_k$.
The fourth uses the law of total covariance, which holds because conditioning on $\Tinfo$ implicitly conditions on $\Pinfo$;
its second term disappears as founder alleles are independent of their downstream transmissions, so $\E\big[\mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)} \mid \Tinfo\big] = \E\big[\mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)}\big] = 0$.
Note that unlike Equation~\eqref{eq:ibd-founder}, where $\Theta^{(uv)}_{ijk}$ could only take on values of 0 or 1 because the founder alleles were either identical or not, $\widehat\Theta^{(uv)}_{ij}$ can take on intermediate values because it is effectively averaging across the possible combinations of founder alleles each focal allele could have inherited from based on the pedigree.
It follows that expectations of the genome-wide Equations~\eqref{eq:kinship} and~\eqref{eq:inbreeding} given the pedigree are:
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:pedigree-coefs}
\begin{align}
    \widehat\Theta_{ij} \;&=\; \E[\Theta_{ij} \mid \Pinfo] \;=\; \frac14 \sum_{u,v} \widehat\Theta^{(uv)}_{ij} \label{eq:kinship-hat} \\
    \widehat{f}_i \;&=\; \E[f_i \mid \Pinfo] \;=\; \widehat\Theta^{(mp)}_{ii} \;=\; \widehat\Theta_{m(i)p(i)} \label{eq:inbreeding-hat}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

Equation~\eqref{eq:inbreeding-hat} reveals that the expected inbreeding coefficient of an offspring is equivalent to the expected coefficient of kinship of their parents.

\subsubsection{Expected IBD given recent pedigree}\label{sec:ibd-pedigree}

Now we apply the single-generation building block we just derived to any arbitrary recent pedigree.
The notation and terminology here is admittedly dense because we are not yet assuming a ``well-behaved'' pedigree;
the next section introduces and assumes it, resulting in much simpler formulae.


Recall from Section~\ref{sec:coalescence} that the (realized) transmission chain $\Tchain^{(t)}(\mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)})$ starts from the ancestral allele of generation $t$ down to the \emph{focal allele} $\mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)}$.
We can represent it by a vector enumerating the transmission states ($m$ or $p$) of its meioses.
For some ancestral generation $t$, the \emph{possible transmission chains} $\Tset^{(t)}$ of a focal allele are, as the name implies, all the transmission chains that would be valid given some set of transmission states.
As a result, they are a property of pedigree information, not transmission information, and are the same for every variant, so we drop the variant index $k$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:possible-tchains}
    \Tset^{(t)}\big(\mathring{x}_i^{(u)}\big) \;:=\; \{m,p\}^{t_i - t} ,
    \qquad
    \Tchain^{(t)}\big(\mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)}\big) \in \Tset^{(t)}\big(\mathring{x}_i^{(u)}\big)
\end{equation}
There are $2^{t_i - t}$ possible transmission chains connecting a focal allele of generation $t_i$ to the ancestral alleles of generation $t$, regardless of how inbred the pedigree is (under \assumptionid{Discrete-generations}).
However, inbreeding causes different transmission chains to coalesce onto the same allele, meaning the number of unique possible allele sources at generation $t$ is less than $2^{t_i - t}$.
Under \assumptionid{Independent-assortment}, each meiosis on a possible transmission chain transmits that allele with probability $\tfrac12$, so the probability of a possible transmission chain being realized is $2^{-(t_i - t)}$.

Likewise, the \emph{possible coalescence paths} of two focal alleles with a source in generation $t$, $\Cset^{(t)}$, are the pairs of their possible transmission chains that have the same source and share no other allele.
The set of all possible coalescence paths is just the union across all recent generations, $t \geq t_B$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:possible-cpaths}
\begin{aligned}
    \Cset^{(t)}\big(\mathring{x}_i^{(u)}, \mathring{x}_j^{(v)}\big)
    \;&:=\; \Big\{\, \Cpath = \big\{\Tchain_1, \Tchain_2\big\} \;:\;
    \Tchain_1 \in \Tset^{(t)}\big(\mathring{x}_i^{(u)}\big),\ \
    \Tchain_2 \in \Tset^{(t)}\big(\mathring{x}_j^{(v)}\big), \\
    &\quad \Tchain_1 \text{ and } \Tchain_2 \text{ share a source and no other allele} \,\Big\} \\[4pt]
    \Cset\big(\mathring{x}_i^{(u)}, \mathring{x}_j^{(v)}\big)
    \;&:=\; \bigcup_{t_B \,\leq\, t \,\leq\, \min(t_i, t_j)} \Cset^{(t)}\big(\mathring{x}_i^{(u)}, \mathring{x}_j^{(v)}\big)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
A possible coalescence path with a source in generation $t$ has $|\Cpath| = (t_i - t) + (t_j - t)$ meioses.
Furthermore, we make the notation flexible by allowing the $(u)$ and $(v)$ superscripts to be omitted, thus defining possible coalescence paths at the level of the individuals possessing the sink alleles:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:possible-cpaths-ind}
    \Cset(i,j) \;:=\; \bigsqcup_{u,v \in \{m,p\}} \Cset\big(\mathring{x}_i^{(u)}, \mathring{x}_j^{(v)}\big)
\end{equation}
The union is disjoint ($\sqcup$), keeping a path once for every pair $(u,v)$ it belongs to.
This only matters when $i = j$, where each path between $i$'s two alleles belongs to both $(u,v) = (m,p)$ and $(p,m)$.

Under \assumptionid{Outbred-founders}, two alleles are IBD exactly when their realized transmission chains are such that one of the possible coalescence paths is realized.
Because the set of meioses in each of the two transmission chains of a coalescence path are non-overlapping, the probability of each being realized is independent across the two alleles:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ibd-cpaths}
\begin{aligned}
    \widehat\Theta^{(uv)}_{ij}
    \;&=\; \sum_{\Cpath \,\in\, \Cset(\mathring{x}_i^{(u)}, \mathring{x}_j^{(v)})} \Pr\big[\Cpath \text{ is realized} \;\big|\; \Pinfo\big] \\
    \;&=\; \sum_{\Cpath \,\in\, \Cset(\mathring{x}_i^{(u)}, \mathring{x}_j^{(v)})} 2^{-|\Cpath|} .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The expected coefficient of kinship between two individuals in a pedigree is therefore a sum over the possible coalescence paths between their alleles, each weighted by its length:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kinship-cpaths}
\begin{aligned}
    \widehat\Theta_{ij} \;&=\; \frac14 \sum_{u,v \in \{m,p\}}\ \ \sum_{\Cpath \,\in\, \Cset(\mathring{x}_i^{(u)}, \mathring{x}_j^{(v)})} 2^{-|\Cpath|} \\
    \;&=\; \frac14 \sum_{\Cpath \,\in\, \Cset(i,j)} 2^{-|\Cpath|}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Expected IBD given well-behaved pedigree}\label{sec:ibd-well-behaved}

The previous section derived the expected relatedness of two individuals in a recent pedigree by enumerating all the possible ways that the two individuals could have inherited the same alleles.
When the recent pedigree of two individuals is ``well-behaved'', this set of possible coalescence paths typically becomes constrained, and we can derive a simpler formula.
The term \emph{well-behaved pedigree} is somewhat arbitrary and follows from wanting simpler formulae to begin with.
The first of the next two assumptions (in conjunction with \assumptionid{Outbred-founders}) is sufficient for a pedigree to be outbred, but the second assumption is also needed to make a pedigree well-behaved.
Appendix~\ref{app:badly-behaved} gives examples of pedigrees that are outbred but nonetheless badly-behaved, justifying the need for the second assumption.
\begin{assumption}{Unrelated-mates}{Unrelated mates}
    No mate pairs in the pedigree share a recent common ancestor.
\related{no inbreeding; outbred pedigree; together with \assumptionid{Outbred-founders} (Section~\ref{sec:coalescence}), makes a pedigree outbred.}
\end{assumption}
We use $\hat{t}_{ij} \in \mathbb{Z}$ to denote the most recent generation for which there is a recent common ancestor of $i$ and $j$.
Then, we define the \textbf{most recent common ancestors (MRCAs)} of $i$ and $j$, denoted by $\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{ij}$, to be the set of generation-$\hat{t}_{ij}$ common ancestors of $i$ and $j$.
For our purposes, if $j$ is an ancestor of $i$, then $\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{ij} = \{j \}$, meaning if $i=j$ (a special case of ``lineal relatives''), then $\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{ii} = \{i \}$.
\begin{assumption}{Max-MRCAs}{At most two most recent common ancestors}
    Any two individuals $i$ and $j$ have at most two MRCAs.
    If they have two MRCAs, each of $i$ and $j$ must have an ancestor who is a child of both MRCAs.
    The number of MRCAs, $|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{ij}|$, is therefore:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item 0 if $i$ and $j$ are unrelated;
        \item 1 if they are \textbf{half-relatives} or lineal relatives;
        \item 2 if they are \textbf{full-relatives}.
    \end{itemize}
    Every other recent common ancestor of $i$ and $j$ must be an ancestor of one of their MRCAs.
    \related{no double relatives (e.g., double first cousins).}
\end{assumption}

These assumptions heavily simplify computing expected IBD between two probands because they constrain the form of possible recent coalescence paths between them.
Specifically, all possible recent coalescence paths can only differ in 1) which MRCA they source from if they have two, and 2) which of the two alleles of that MRCA they source from.
For completeness, we express this constraint using set notation.

We write a possible transmission chain from its source in generation $\hat{t}_{ij}$ down to its sink (one of $i$'s alleles) as a vector of transmission states ordered from oldest to youngest, $(u_{\hat{t}_{ij}}, u_{\hat{t}_{ij}+1}, \dots, u_{t_i}) \in \{m,p\}^{t_i - \hat{t}_{ij} + 1}$.
Each entry $u_t$ is the transmission state of the meiosis inherited by the generation-$t$ individual on the chain, i.e., which of their parents they received the chain's allele from.
This usage of $u$ (and $v$ when discussing $j$'s transmission chain) matches its use as a superscript denoting the parent-of-origin of an allele, e.g., $x^{(u)}_{i,k}$, and as a shorthand to denote the parent of someone, which can be used recursively, e.g., $u_{t_i - 1}(u_{t_i}(i))$ where $u_{t_i - 1} = m$ and $u_{t_i} = p$ denotes individual $i$'s paternal grandmother.
Under this notation, we can express the possible coalescence paths $\Cset(i,j)$ as the pairs of transmission chains following two constraints:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:well-behaved-cpaths}
\begin{aligned}
    \Cset(i,j) \;=\; \Big\{\, & \big\{ (u_{\hat{t}_{ij}}, \dots, u_{t_i}),\ (v_{\hat{t}_{ij}}, \dots, v_{t_j}) \big\} \;: \\
    & \text{(1)}\ \ u_{\hat{t}_{ij}+1}\big(\cdots u_{t_i}(i)\big) \;=\; v_{\hat{t}_{ij}+1}\big(\cdots v_{t_j}(j)\big) \;\in\; \widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{ij}, \\
    & \text{(2)}\ \ u_{\hat{t}_{ij}} \;=\; v_{\hat{t}_{ij}} \,\Big\}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
That is, all possible coalescence paths of a pair must coalesce at an MRCA of the two (constraint 1) and the allele they transmitted down must be from the same side of their family (constraint 2).
An additional consequence of the first constraint is that the second-oldest allele of each chain (defined when $t_i > \hat{t}_{ij}$ and $t_j > \hat{t}_{ij}$) must also have the same parent-of-origin (the sex of the MRCA):
$$
    u_{\hat{t}_{ij}+1} \;=\; v_{\hat{t}_{ij}+1} \;\in\;
    \begin{cases}
        \{m, p\} & \text{full-relatives} \\
        \{m\} & \text{maternal half-relatives} \\
        \{p\} & \text{paternal half-relatives}
    \end{cases}
$$
All other transmission states in the coalescence path ($t > \hat{t}_{ij} + 1$) are fixed by the pedigree, since under \assumptionid{Unrelated-mates}, each proband descends from each MRCA along a single lineal chain.
Therefore, transmission information beyond what the pedigree captures only informs us of which of the possible coalescence paths are realized (if any).

Figure~\ref{fig:ibd-cousins} below shows the possible coalescence paths of full first cousins, identified by tracing the arrows up and then down the graph between the two cousins' alleles.
Four such possible coalescence paths exist, each of length 4.
The possible coalescence paths between any pair of descendants of each cousin (assuming a well-behaved pedigree) simply extend the beginning and end of a possible coalescence path listed here, with transmission states that are fixed by the pedigree.

We now return to the generalized coefficient of kinship formula we derived in Section~\ref{sec:ibd-pedigree} (Equation~\eqref{eq:kinship-cpaths}), which our constraints simplify.
Following from \assumptionid{Max-MRCAs}, the number of possible coalescence paths is either zero, $|\Cset(i,j)| = 0$, if $i$ and $j$ are unrelated, $|\Cset(i,j)| = 2$ if $i$ and $j$ are half-relatives or lineal relatives, or $|\Cset(i,j)| = 4$ if $i$ and $j$ are full-relatives.
Furthermore, each possible coalescence path of a pair is of the same length, since every MRCA belongs to generation $\hat{t}_{ij}$, which we'll denote by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cpath-length}
    |\Cpath(i,j)| \;:=\; (t_i - \hat{t}_{ij}) + (t_j - \hat{t}_{ij})
    \qquad \text{for all } \Cpath(i,j) \in \Cset(i,j)
\end{equation}
Under these constraints:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kinship-well-behaved}
\begin{aligned}
    \widehat\Theta_{ij} \;&=\; \frac14 \sum_{\Cpath \,\in\, \Cset(i,j)} 2^{-|\Cpath|} \\
    \;&=\; \frac14 \, |\Cset(i,j)| \; 2^{- |\Cpath(i,j)|}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

We define the \textbf{genetic degree} between individuals $i$ and $j$, $\ddeg_{ij} \in \R_{\geq 0} \cup \{\infty\}$, to be the length of their possible coalescence paths, minus one if $i$ and $j$ are full-relatives:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:genetic-degree}
    \ddeg_{ij} \;:=\;
    \begin{cases}
        |\Cpath(i,j)| - 1 & \text{full-relatives} \\
        |\Cpath(i,j)| & \text{half-relatives or lineal relatives} \\
        \infty & \text{unrelated}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This quantity is useful because it lets us express the expected coefficient of kinship between two individuals in a well-behaved pedigree as simply:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kinship-degree}
    \widehat\Theta_{ij} \;=\; \tfrac12\, 2^{-\ddeg_{ij}}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, we can express the genetic degree as a function of the coefficient of kinship (or relatedness, $\widehat\pi_{ij} = 2\widehat\Theta_{ij}$):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:degree-kinship}
    \ddeg_{ij} \;=\; -\log_2\!\big(\widehat\Theta_{ij}\big) - 1 \;=\; -\log_2 (\widehat\pi_{ij})
\end{equation}
with $-\log_2 0 = \infty$ for unrelated pairs.

\section{Additive genetics under random mating}\label{sec:random-mating}

In this section, we assume a simple trait architecture and population dynamics and derive how phenotypic covariance between two individuals changes based on the information we have about the pair and the trait.

To recap, we assume \emph{additive genetics model with independent environment}.
That is, the phenotype is a sum of a genetic value and environmental values (\assumptionid{Additive-phenotype}) which are independent within and across individuals (\assumptionid{GE-indep-within}, \assumptionid{GE-indep-across}, \assumptionid{Env-indep}), with the exception that alleles are transmitted from parent to offspring (\assumptionid{Segregation}) independently across variants (\assumptionid{Independent-assortment}).
Genetic effects are linear with allele count (\assumptionid{Linearity}).
All other assumptions stated so far also apply.

\subsubsection{Random mating}\label{sec:rm-random-mating}

In this section, we use the simplest mating model: \textbf{random mating}.

\begin{assumption}{Random-mating}{Random mating}
The probability of two individuals mating with each other is constant across all pairs of mating individuals.
Using $\mathcal{M}$ to denote the set of all individuals in a generation that mate:

$$ \Pr\big[\dmu_{ij} = 1 \;\big|\; i, j \in \mathcal{M}\big] \ \text{ is the same for every pair } i \neq j . $$
Under monogamy, this probability is $(|\mathcal{M}| - 1)^{-1}$.
We also assume the distribution of the number of offspring per mate pair is identical for all mate pairs.
\related{no population structure, no assortative mating, no migration, no modeling of sex.}
\end{assumption}

Previously, we showed that individual centered alleles have variance $\Var[x^{(u)}_{ik}] = p_k q_k$.
However, computing the variance of (centered) genotypes, $x_{ik} = x^{(m)}_{ik} + x^{(p)}_{ik}$ requires knowledge of $\Cov[x^{(m)}_{ik},x^{(p)}_{ik}]$.
Extending from Section~\ref{sec:ibd-transmission} (Equation~\eqref{eq:ibd-founder}), we know that if two alleles are IBD, then they are perfectly correlated (covariance of $p_k q_k$).
If the two alleles are not IBD, then they derive from independent founder alleles and are therefore uncorrelated (covariance of $0$).
We can simplify the covariance to equal $p_k q_k$ times the probability of the two alleles being IBD:
$$
\Cov[x^{(m)}_{ik},x^{(p)}_{ik}] = p_k q_k \;
\E[ \IBD(x^{(m)}_{ik},x^{(p)}_{ik}) ]
$$
Since $\IBD(x^{(m)}_{ik},x^{(p)}_{ik}) = f_{ik}$, we can also write this in terms of the inbreeding coefficient (Equation~\eqref{eq:inbreeding}):
$$
\Cov[x^{(m)}_{ik},x^{(p)}_{ik}] = p_k q_k \; \E[ f_{ik}]
$$
We could simply assume \assumptionid{Unrelated-mates}, which would mean that mates are unrelated, therefore meaning that $\E[ f_{ik}] = \Cov[x^{(m)}_{ik},x^{(p)}_{ik}] = 0$.
However, we'll be more thorough and instead establish that random mating approximately results in \assumptionid{Unrelated-mates} given a safer new assumption:

\begin{assumption}{Large-Ne}{Large effective population size}
Define the \textbf{effective population size}, $N_e \in \R_{>0}$, as half the inverse of the probability of two alleles chosen at random from different individuals of the same generation being IBD, such that:
$$ \E\big[\IBD(x^{(u)}_{ik}, x^{(v)}_{jk})\big] \;=\; \frac{1}{2 N_e} \qquad (i \neq j) . $$
We assume $N_e$ is large for the purposes of downstream approximations.
\related{no population bottleneck.}
\end{assumption}

\begin{approximation}{Random-mates-unrelated}{Random mates are approximately unrelated}
Random mating results in roughly unrelated mates and therefore outbred offspring:
$$ \Cov[x^{(m)}_{ik}, x^{(p)}_{ik}] \;=\; p_k q_k \E[ f_{ik}] \;=\; \frac{p_k q_k}{2 N_e} \;\approx\; 0 . $$
\justification{Two alleles chosen at random from different individuals have a $\frac{1}{2 N_e}$ probability of being IBD.
The offspring of a randomly-mated pair (\assumptionid{Random-mating}) effectively inherits two such alleles from the preceding generation, so $\E[ f_{ik}] = \frac{1}{2 N_e}$, which is small given a large enough $N_e$ (\assumptionid{Large-Ne}).}
\end{approximation}

Note that we can also make the reverse argument: random mating with the additional constraint of forcing parents to be unrelated still approximately looks like true random mating. 
We can now confidently state the variance of genotypes under random mating as being double that of alleles:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:var-x}
\begin{aligned}
    \Var[x_{ik}]
    \;&=\; \Var\big[x^{(m)}_{ik}\big] + \Var\big[x^{(p)}_{ik}\big] + 2\Cov\big[x^{(m)}_{ik}, x^{(p)}_{ik}\big] \\
    \;&=\; 2 p_k q_k + 2 p_k q_k\, \E[f_{ik}] \\
    \;&=\; 2 p_k q_k \Big(1 + \frac{1}{2 N_e}\Big) \\
    \;&\approx\; 2 p_k q_k
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, standardized genotypes (Equation~\eqref{eq:standardized}) have approximately unit variance, $\Var[\tilde{x}_{ik}] \approx 1$.

\subsubsection{Cross-products}

Because our phenotypes are zero-centered, the conditional covariance in phenotypic values between two individuals is equal to the expected conditional cross product of their phenotypes.
The same applies to other centered variables.
Using $\Iinfo_{ij}$ as a shorthand for any information about a pair, we can write:
$$ \E\big[y_i y_j \mid \Iinfo_{ij}\big] \;=\; \Cov\big[y_i, y_j \mid \Iinfo_{ij}\big] $$

Furthermore, due to gene--environment independence and random mating, expected phenotypic cross-products are equivalent to expected genetic value cross-products when $i \neq j$:
$$ \E[y_i y_j] \;=\; \E\big[(g_i + e_i)(g_j + e_j)\big] \;=\; \E[g_i g_j] \qquad (i \neq j) $$
When $i = j$, $\E[y_i^2] = \Var[y_i] = V_Y$.

\subsection{Covariance given genotypes and effect sizes}\label{sec:rm-cov-geno-effects}

Suppose we have genotypic information about a pair, $\Ginfo$.
Specifically, we have their genotype matrix, $\mathbf{G}$ (non-centered) and $\bX$ (centered), for $M$ genotyped variants, which include all causal variants for the trait of interest.
Suppose we also know the effect sizes of all the genotyped variants, $\bbeta$.
Furthermore, we adopt stronger versions of the \assumptionid{GE-indep-within} and \assumptionid{GE-indep-across} assumptions, assuming independence between environmental values and jointly-distributed genotypes and effect sizes.

\begin{assumption}{GE-joint-indep-within}{Genotype--environment independence within individuals}
An individual's environmental value is jointly independent of their own genotypes and alleles and effect sizes:
$$ \big(\mathbf{G}_i, \mathbf{G}^{(u)}_i, \bbeta\big) \;\perp\; e_i . $$
\related{no gene--environment correlation within individuals, no population stratification, implies \assumptionid{GE-indep-within} (Section~\ref{sec:pheno-form}).}
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}{GE-joint-indep-across}{Genotype--environment independence across individuals}
An individual's environmental value is jointly independent of another individual's genotypes and alleles and effect sizes:
$$ \big(\mathbf{G}_j, \mathbf{G}^{(u)}_j, \bbeta\big) \;\perp\; e_i \ \ (i \neq j) . $$
\related{no gene--environment correlation across individuals, no indirect (e.g., parental) genetic effects, no population stratification, implies \assumptionid{GE-indep-across} (Section~\ref{sec:pheno-form}).}
\end{assumption}
Note that \assumptionid{GE-joint-indep-within} is a much stronger constraint on population stratification compared to the weaker constraint imposed in \assumptionid{GE-indep-within}, which requires strong enough population structure, migration, and/or differential selection to cause large differences in mean genetic values across subpopulations.
A more thorough discussion is reserved for the population structure and stratification sections of this document.

It is now trivial to derive the expected phenotypic cross-product conditional on this information ($i \neq j$):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cov-geno-effects}
\begin{aligned}
    \E\big[y_i y_j \mid \bX, \bbeta\big]
    \;&=\; \E\big[g_i g_j \mid \bX, \bbeta\big] \\
    \;&=\; \E\bigg[\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{M} \beta_k\, x_{ik}\Big) \Big(\sum_{k'=1}^{M} \beta_{k'}\, x_{jk'}\Big) \;\bigg|\; \bX, \bbeta\bigg] \\
    \;&=\; \sum_{k=1}^{M} \sum_{k'=1}^{M} \beta_k \beta_{k'}\, x_{ik}\, x_{jk'}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The first step follows from \assumptionid{GE-joint-indep-within} and \assumptionid{GE-joint-indep-across}, which let us drop the conditioning on $\bX$ and $\bbeta$ from every term containing an environmental value, and \assumptionid{Env-indep}, which then makes $\E[e_i e_j] = 0$.

\subsection{Covariance given genotypes}\label{sec:rm-cov-geno}

\subsubsection{Effect size distribution assumptions}\label{sec:effect-size-dist}

In practice, we often do not know the effect sizes of variants.
We avoid making unnecessary assumptions about these effect size distributions and instead only list the assumptions needed to make the immediate downstream claims we're interested in.

We have already constrained the first moment of effect sizes to be zero (\assumptionid{Effects-first-moment-zero}), but we must also constrain the second moment:

\begin{assumption}{Effects-second-moment-var-constant}{Squared effect sizes scale with allele frequency}
Averaged over variants with the same allele frequency, the squared effect size is a constant, $\sigma_\beta^2 \in \R_{>0}$, scaled by a power, $\alpha \in \R$, of the allele-frequency term used to standardize genotypes, $2p_k q_k$:
$$ \E\big[\beta_k^2 \mid p_k\big] \;=\; \E\big[\fbeta(\Binfo_k) \mid p_k\big] \;=\; \sigma_\beta^2 \, \big[2p_k q_k\big]^{\alpha} . $$
\related{the infinitesimal / random-effects model.}
\end{assumption}

 For $\alpha < 0$, the allele-frequency term implies that rarer variants will tend to have larger squared effect sizes than common variants.

\assumptionid{Effects-second-moment-var-constant} makes the strong assumption that the relationship between effect size and allele frequency (which is primarily driven by selection, a functional property) is completely accounted for by the above form.

\begin{assumption}{Effects-second-moment-covar}{Effect sizes are uncorrelated across variants}
In conjunction with \assumptionid{Effects-first-moment-zero}, the effect sizes of two different variants have zero expected cross-product:
$$ \E[\beta_k \beta_{k'} \mid p_k, p_{k'}] \;=\; 0 \qquad (k \neq k') . $$
\related{the infinitesimal / random-effects model, whose effects are drawn independently across variants.}
\end{assumption}

Next, we need to assume that individual genotype information is not predictive of variant effect sizes up to the second moment.
Any form of non-random mating or selection that is downstream of effect sizes violates this assumption;
later sections of this document explicitly try to model this relationship.

\begin{assumption}{Effects-genotype-independence}{Effect sizes are independent of genotypes}
Conditioning on the genotypes leaves the first and second moments of effect sizes unchanged, beyond the allele frequencies the genotypes reveal:
$$ \E[\beta_k \mid \bX] \;=\; \E[\beta_k \mid p_k], \qquad \E[\beta_k \beta_{k'} \mid \bX] \;=\; \E[\beta_k \beta_{k'} \mid p_k, p_{k'}] $$
\related{no assortative mating, no selection, no population stratification.}
\end{assumption}

\subsubsection{Covariance given genotypes and exchangeable variants}

With these assumptions, we can now take the expectation of Equation~\eqref{eq:cov-geno-effects} over the effect sizes, using the law of total expectation and then \assumptionid{Effects-genotype-independence}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cov-geno-sum}
\begin{aligned}
    \E\big[g_i g_j \mid \bX\big]
    \;&=\; \E\Big[\, \E\big[g_i g_j \mid \bX, \bbeta\big] \;\Big|\; \bX \Big] \\
    \;&=\; \sum_{k=1}^{M} \sum_{k'=1}^{M} \E\big[\beta_k \beta_{k'} \mid \bX\big]\, x_{ik}\, x_{jk'} \\
    \;&=\; \sum_{k=1}^{M} \sum_{k'=1}^{M} \E\big[\beta_k \beta_{k'} \mid p_k, p_{k'}\big]\, x_{ik}\, x_{jk'}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
By \assumptionid{Effects-second-moment-covar}, all off-diagonal terms ($k\neq k'$) have an expected cross-product of 0, even if the genotypes themselves are correlated with each other.
Subsequently, only main diagonal terms ($k = k'$) remain, weighted by each variant's squared effect size, which by \assumptionid{Effects-second-moment-var-constant} depends only on its allele frequency:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cov-geno-diag}
    \E\big[g_i g_j \mid \bX\big] \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{M} \E\big[\beta_k^2 \mid p_k\big]\, x_{ik}\, x_{jk}
    \;=\; \sigma_\beta^2 \sum_{k=1}^{M} \big[2p_k q_k\big]^{\alpha}\, x_{ik}\, x_{jk}
\end{equation}
To relate this to genotypic similarity (Equation~\eqref{eq:grm}), we now fix $\alpha$:

\begin{assumption}{Effects-alpha-minus-one}{Standardized effect sizes have the same variance}
The allele-frequency exponent of \assumptionid{Effects-second-moment-var-constant} is $\alpha = -1$:
$$ \E\big[\beta_k^2 \mid p_k\big] \;=\; \sigma_\beta^2 \, \big[2p_k q_k\big]^{-1} . $$
Equivalently, every variant has the same expected squared \emph{standardized} effect size (Equation~\eqref{eq:standardized-effect}), $\E[\tilde{\beta}_k^2 \mid p_k] = \sigma_\beta^2$.
\related{equal per-standardized-allele effect variance across variants.}
\end{assumption}

With these assumptions, the variants are \emph{second-order exchangeable} with regards to their standardized effect sizes, meaning their pairwise joint distributions are identical up to the second moment.
The choice of $\alpha = -1$ is primarily driven by its wide adoption in the literature for developing theory, despite specific empirical studies clearly showing $\alpha \neq -1$ \citep{Schoech2019NatComm}.

Previously in Equation~\eqref{eq:additive-variance}, we introduced the additive genetic variance as the variance of genetic values in the population, $V_A := \Var[g_i \mid \Ginfo, \bbeta]$. 
If we don't condition on effect sizes nor genotypes and instead average over them, we can derive the expected additive variance under random mating.
Furthermore, previously in Equation~\eqref{eq:var-x}, we derived that $\Var[x_{ik}] \approx 2p_k q_k$, and in Equation~\eqref{eq:cov-geno-diag} that the off-diagonal terms go to zero:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:additive-variance-effects}
\begin{aligned}
    \E[V_A]
    \;&=\; \E\Big[\Var\big[g_i \mid \bX, \bbeta\big]\Big] \\
    \;&=\; \Var[g_i] \;-\; \Var\Big[\E\big[g_i \mid \bX, \bbeta\big]\Big] \\
    \;&\approx\; \Var[g_i] \;=\; \E\big[g_i^2\big] \\
    \;&=\; \E\Big[\, \E\big[g_i g_i \mid \bX\big] \Big] \\
    \;&=\; \E\bigg[\, \sum_{k=1}^{M} \E\big[\beta_k^2 \mid p_k\big]\, x_{ik}^2 \bigg] \\
    \;&=\; \sum_{k=1}^{M} \E\big[\beta_k^2 \mid p_k\big]\, \E\big[x_{ik}^2\big] \\
    \;&\approx\; \sum_{k=1}^{M} \sigma_\beta^2 \big[2p_k q_k\big]^{-1}\, 2p_k q_k \;=\; M \sigma_\beta^2
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The second line is the law of total variance, and the following approximation assumes that the population's mean genetic value, $\E[g_i \mid \bX, \bbeta]$, is approximately zero.
Because we assume \assumptionid{Large-Ne}, realized allele frequencies are close enough to their expectation, $p_k$, such that this is a safe approximation.
The last approximation follows from Equation~\eqref{eq:var-x} and \assumptionid{Effects-alpha-minus-one}.

Under \assumptionid{Effects-second-moment-var-constant}, \assumptionid{Effects-alpha-minus-one}, and \assumptionid{Random-mating}, the expected additive variance is therefore distributed uniformly across all second-order exchangeable variants.
Note that the realized $V_A$ is not itself equal to $M\sigma_\beta^2$ because covariance between genotypes (such as arising from random chance) prevents the off-diagonal terms from being dropped from the above equation.
However, under random mating and with many variants, the two are approximately equal:

\begin{approximation}{Many-variants}{The additive variance is close to its expectation}
With many causal variants, the additive variance is close to its expectation:
$$ V_A \;\approx\; \E[V_A] \;\approx\; M\sigma_\beta^2 , \qquad \text{so that} \qquad \sigma_\beta^2 \;\approx\; \frac{V_A}{M} . $$
\justification{Given the population's genotypes, $V_A = \sum_{k}\sum_{k'} \beta_k \beta_{k'} \Cov[x_{ik}, x_{ik'} \mid \bX]$.
Averaging over variants removes the off-diagonal terms and keeps the $M$ diagonal terms, each with expectation $\E[\beta_k^2 \mid p_k] \Var[x_{ik} \mid \bX] \approx \sigma_\beta^2$.
If standardized effect sizes are distributed with a kurtosis $\kappa := \E[\tilde{\beta}_k^4]/\sigma_\beta^4$, then variance around this expectation itself is:
$$ \frac{\Var\big[V_A \mid \bX\big]}{\E\big[V_A \mid \bX\big]^2} \;\approx\; \frac{\kappa - 3 + 2\bar{\ell}}{M}, \qquad \bar{\ell} \;:=\; \frac{1}{M}\sum_{k}\sum_{k'} \Corr[x_{ik}, x_{ik'} \mid \bX]^2 , $$
where $\bar{\ell}$ is the average, across variants, of a variant's summed squared genotype correlations with every variant (i.e., the mean linkage disequilibrium (LD) score, discussed more later).
As the number of variants, $M$, grows relative to their average LD ($\bar{\ell}$) and to the kurtosis of effect sizes ($\kappa$), this variance goes to zero, making the approximation $V_A \approx \E[V_A \mid \bX]$ more accurate.
For reference, normally distributed effect sizes have a kurtosis of $\kappa = 3$.
}
\related{the infinitesimal model.}
\end{approximation}

Therefore:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cov-geno-va}
\begin{aligned}
    \E\big[y_i y_j \mid \bX\big]
    \;&=\; \E\big[g_i g_j \mid \bX\big] \\
    \;&=\; \sum_{k=1}^{M} \sum_{k'=1}^{M} \E\big[\beta_k \beta_{k'} \mid p_k, p_{k'}\big]\, x_{ik}\, x_{jk'} \\
    \;&=\; \sum_{k=1}^{M} \E\big[\beta_k^2 \mid p_k\big]\, x_{ik}\, x_{jk}
    \;+\; \sum_{k \neq k'} \underbrace{\E\big[\beta_k \beta_{k'} \mid p_k, p_{k'}\big]}_{=\,0}\, x_{ik}\, x_{jk'} \\
    \;&=\; \sigma_\beta^2 \sum_{k=1}^{M} \frac{x_{ik}\, x_{jk}}{2p_k q_k} \\
    \;&\approx\; V_A \cdot \frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M} \frac{x_{ik}\, x_{jk}}{2p_k q_k} .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
In this equation, we also observe genotypic similarity, i.e., an element of the genetic relationship matrix, $A_{ij} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M} \frac{x_{ik}\, x_{jk}}{2p_k q_k}$, which we derived earlier in Equation~\eqref{eq:grm}.
Therefore, we can further simplify:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cov-grm}
    \E\big[y_i y_j \mid A_{ij}\big] \;\approx\; V_A\, A_{ij}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Covariance given relatedness}\label{sec:rm-relatedness}

\subsubsection{Covariance given coefficient of kinship}\label{sec:rm-kinship}
Suppose that for a pair of individuals, we do not know their genotypes, $\mathbf{G} \in \Ginfo$, but do know their coefficient of kinship, $\Theta_{ij} \in \Tinfo$.
We can derive the expected phenotypic cross-product as follows.

Because IBD implies IBS (\assumptionid{No-recent-mutation}), the cross-product of IBD allele pairs is $p_k q_k$.
In contrast, allele pairs that are not IBD must therefore have been inherited from non-overlapping transmission chains.
\assumptionid{Random-mating} ensures that the individuals along those non-overlapping transmission chains are chosen roughly at random from the population, meaning the alleles transmitted along each chain are uncorrelated.
Therefore, without knowledge of genotypes, non-IBD allele pairs have an expected cross-product of $0$. 
We can write this as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:allele-cross-ibd}
    \E\big[x^{(u)}_{ik}\, x^{(v)}_{jk} \;\big|\; \Theta^{(uv)}_{ijk}\big]
    \;=\; \begin{cases}
        p_k q_k & \Theta^{(uv)}_{ijk} = 1 \\
        0 & \Theta^{(uv)}_{ijk} = 0
    \end{cases}
    \;=\; p_k q_k\, \Theta^{(uv)}_{ijk}
\end{equation}

Using the allele-level formula for the genotypic similarity (Equation~\eqref{eq:grm-alleles}), we can derive the expected genotypic similarity given the coefficient of kinship:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:grm-kinship}
\begin{aligned}
    \E\big[A_{ij} \mid \Theta_{ij}\big]
    \;&=\; \frac{1}{M} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \sum_{u,v \in \{m,p\}} \E\big[\tilde{x}^{(u)}_{ik}\, \tilde{x}^{(v)}_{jk} \mid \Theta_{ij}\big] \\
    \;&=\; \frac{1}{M} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \sum_{u,v \in \{m,p\}} \E\Big[\, \E\big[\tilde{x}^{(u)}_{ik}\, \tilde{x}^{(v)}_{jk} \mid \Theta^{(uv)}_{ijk}\big] \;\Big|\; \Theta_{ij}\Big] \\
    \;&=\; \frac{1}{M} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \sum_{u,v \in \{m,p\}} \frac{p_k q_k}{2p_k q_k}\, \E\big[\Theta^{(uv)}_{ijk} \mid \Theta_{ij}\big] \\
    \;&=\; 2\, \E\bigg[\frac{1}{4M} \sum_{k=1}^{M} \sum_{u,v \in \{m,p\}} \Theta^{(uv)}_{ijk} \;\bigg|\; \Theta_{ij}\bigg] \\
    \;&=\; 2\, \Theta_{ij} \;=\; \pi_{ij}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Before proceeding, we need to explicitly state that the coefficient of kinship of two individuals does not give us any information about their phenotypic covariance beyond what their genotypic similarity tells us:

\begin{assumption}{Kinship-screened}{Genotypic similarity screens off kinship}
Given the genotypic similarity, the coefficient of kinship conveys no further information about the phenotypic cross-product:
$$ \E\big[y_i y_j \mid A_{ij}, \Theta_{ij}\big] \;=\; \E\big[y_i y_j \mid A_{ij}\big] . $$
\related{no shared environment among relatives, no indirect (e.g., parental) genetic effects, no effect sizes that depend on relatedness.}
\end{assumption}

With that assumption, we can simplify Equation~\eqref{eq:cov-grm} as follows ($i \neq j$):

\begin{equation}\label{eq:cov-kinship}
\begin{aligned}
    \E\big[y_i y_j \mid \Theta_{ij}\big]
    \;&=\; \E\Big[\, \E\big[y_i y_j \mid A_{ij}, \Theta_{ij}\big] \;\Big|\; \Theta_{ij} \Big] \\
    \;&=\; \E\Big[\, \E\big[y_i y_j \mid A_{ij}\big] \;\Big|\; \Theta_{ij} \Big] \\
    \;&\approx\; V_A\, \E\big[A_{ij} \mid \Theta_{ij}\big] \\
    \;&=\; 2\, V_A\, \Theta_{ij} \\
    \;&=\; V_A\, \pi_{ij}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Covariance given genetic degree}\label{sec:rm-degree}

Suppose that for a pair of individuals, we do not even know their coefficient of kinship, only their genetic degree, $\ddeg_{ij} \in \Pinfo$.
We can take a similar approach as Section~\ref{sec:rm-kinship} to derive the expected phenotypic cross-product.

Earlier in Equation~\eqref{eq:kinship-degree}, we already derived the expected coefficient of kinship given the genetic degree:
$\E[\Theta_{ij} \mid \ddeg_{ij}] = \E\big[\widehat\Theta_{ij} \mid \ddeg_{ij}\big] = \tfrac12\, 2^{-\ddeg_{ij}}$.
Like the \assumptionid{Kinship-screened} assumption of the previous section, we similarly assume:

\begin{assumption}{Degree-screened}{Kinship screens off genetic degree}
Given the coefficient of kinship, the genetic degree conveys no further information about the phenotypic cross-product:
$$ \E\big[y_i y_j \mid \Theta_{ij}, \ddeg_{ij}\big] \;=\; \E\big[y_i y_j \mid \Theta_{ij}\big] . $$
\related{no shared environment among relatives, no indirect (e.g., parental) genetic effects, no effect sizes that depend on relative pedigree position.}
\end{assumption}

We can now simplify Equation~\eqref{eq:cov-kinship} to obtain ($i \neq j$):

\begin{equation}\label{eq:cov-degree}
\begin{aligned}
    \E\big[y_i y_j \mid \ddeg_{ij}\big]
    \;&=\; \E\Big[\, \E\big[y_i y_j \mid \Theta_{ij}, \ddeg_{ij}\big] \;\Big|\; \ddeg_{ij} \Big] \\
    \;&=\; \E\Big[\, \E\big[y_i y_j \mid \Theta_{ij}\big] \;\Big|\; \ddeg_{ij} \Big] \\
    \;&\approx\; 2\, V_A\, \E\big[\Theta_{ij} \mid \ddeg_{ij}\big] \\
    \;&=\; V_A\, 2^{-\ddeg_{ij}}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Hierarchy of information}
Here, we summarize the interesting property that emerges from our assumptions and derivations.
Namely, there is a clear ``hierarchy'' of information with regards to the granularity in phenotypic cross-products it provides:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item The genetic degree, $\ddeg \in Pinfo$, provides the least granularity, assigning the same expected cross-product to everyone with the same genetic degree.
    \item The coefficient of kinship, $\Theta \in \Tinfo$, provides additional granularity around the degree, allowing for more precise expectations between relative pairs of differing IBD proportions.
    \item The genotypic similarity/GRM, $\mathbf{A} \in \Ginfo$, adds further granularity around the kinship-based expectation, broadly differentiating pairs whose non-IBD genome segments happen to be more or less similar.
    \item Finally, effect sizes, $\bbeta \in \Binfo$, provide the highest possible granularity around the genotypic similarity, broadly differentiating pairs whose genotypes happen to be more or less similar at high-effect-size variants.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Additive genetics under assortative mating}\label{sec:am}

\subsection{The mating model}\label{sec:am-model}
We now explicitly revoke the \assumptionid{Random-mating} assumption.
In its place, we adopt a mating model of \textbf{primary assortative mating}:

\begin{assumption}{Primary-AM}{Primary assortative mating}
Mating is non-random such that the phenotypes of two mates, $m$ and $p$, are correlated for some trait at the time of mating:
$$ \Corr[\,y_m, y_p\,] = \rho_y . $$
\related{primary phenotypic assortment, as opposed to social homogamy (matching on a shared environment) or genetic homogamy (matching on genotype directly)}
\end{assumption}
Primary assortative mating means that mates are paired up based directly on their phenotypes, and any other correlations between mates' values emerge from their association with the phenotype.
For example, as we'll soon see, mates' genetic values also become correlated under primary assortative mating, but only because genetic values are correlated with the phenotype mates are matching on.
Following the convention that a subscript names the component being correlated across mates, we write $\rho_g$ for the induced correlation between mates' genetic values:
$ \Corr[\,g_m, g_p\,] = \rho_g $.
Note that $\rho_y$ is often written as $\mu$ or $r$ in the literature (not to be confused with our placeholder co-path coefficient $\mu$, Equation~\eqref{eq:copath-mu}), and it is referred to as the \emph{strength} of assortative mating.

Furthermore, \textbf{convergence} describes the phenomenon where two mates' phenotypic values become more similar after mating, presumably due to each individual affecting the other's environment in some sense.
Our definition of primary assortative mating makes no claims about convergence, since we impose the $\rho_y$ constraint at the time of mating.
However, throughout this section, we will derive formulas for the covariance between phenotypes across individuals, often of different generations.
To avoid having to couch every statement with the clause ``at the time of mating'', we will explicitly assume away convergence:

\begin{assumption}{No-convergence}{No phenotypic convergence after mating}
The correlation in mates' phenotypic values does not change after mating.
\related{no cultural transmission or IGEs between mates.}
\end{assumption}

\subsection{Dynamics and equilibrium}\label{sec:am-dynamics}

The consequences of assortative mating on the subsequent generation's genetics accumulate across generations.
Luckily, a stable equilibrium is eventually reached, which will be the focus of our derivations regarding phenotypic covariance between individuals in Section~\ref{sec:am-rel-values}.
However, first we must establish how that equilibrium is reached, which we will do by starting with a randomly-mating population that then switches to primary assortative mating at generation $t=0$.
Parameters that are time-varying due to the dynamics of assortative mating will contain a $(t)$ superscript to denote the generation they belong to, such as $\rho_g^{(t)}$ and $V_A^{(t)}$.
Note that $\rho_y$ does not change across generations as the \assumptionid{Primary-AM} assumption must hold for a fixed strength.
We will then solve for equilibrium and denote its respective parameters using the superscript $(\mathrm{eq})$.

We remind the reader of key assumptions we've previously made that are heavily relied on here.
Namely, we continue to assume that allele frequencies do not change over time (\assumptionid{Constant-freq}) and that variants are transmitted independently of each other (\assumptionid{Independent-assortment}).
Furthermore, we will heavily rely on path analysis, which is properly introduced in Appendix~\ref{app:path-analysis}.

Three key results from Table~\ref{tab:am-pair-between} are highlighted here.

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item $\Cov[\,x_{mk},\, x_{pl}\,] = 4p_k q_k\,p_l q_l\,\beta_k\beta_l\,\rho_y/\VYo \neq 0$, for every pair of variants $k$ and $l$ where $\beta_k , \beta_l \neq 0$ (and if $\rho_y \neq 0$).
    Genotypes at different variants across mates are correlated, with a sign determined by the product of their effect sizes.
    \item $\Cov[\,e_m,\, g_p\,] = \VAo V_E \,\rho_y/\VYo \neq 0$.
    One mate's environment is correlated with the other's genetic value, which breaks \assumptionid{GE-indep-across} and \assumptionid{GE-joint-indep-across}.
    Despite one mate's genotype not causally affecting the other's environment, the two still become correlated due to the phenotypic matching.
    Two mates' environments also become correlated, $\Cov[\,e_m,\, e_p\,] = V_E^2\,\rho_y/\VYo$, breaking \assumptionid{Env-indep}.
    \item\label{item:rhog-hsq} $\rhogo \,=\, \Corr[\,g_m,\, g_p\,] \,=\, \frac{(\VAo)^2 \rho_y/\VYo}{\VAo} \,=\, \rho_y\,\frac{\VAo}{\VYo} \,=\, \rho_y\,\hsqo . $
    Mates' genetic values are correlated according to the strength of assortative mating and the heritability of the sorting trait.
    Equivalently, the numerator $\Cov[\,g_m,\, g_p\,]$ is the sum of the first result over all $M^2$ ordered pairs of variants, weighted by their effect sizes: $\sum_{k}\sum_{l}\beta_k\beta_l \cdot 4p_k q_k\,p_l q_l\,\beta_k\beta_l\,\rho_y/\VYo = \big(\sum_k 2p_k q_k\,\beta_k^2\big)^2\rho_y/\VYo = (\VAo)^2\rho_y/\VYo$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Commonly-used terms}
Before proceeding, we introduce and re-establish notation that clarifies downstream analysis.
Note that for notational consistency, we should also be attaching a ${(t)}$ superscript to every allele, genotype, etc.
However, for the sake of clarity, we omit this.

\paragraph{Disequilibrium coefficients}

For an individual $i$ of generation $t$ and variants $k$ and $l$, we define the \textbf{non-gametic disequilibrium coefficient}, $D^{(t)}_{k/l}$, to be the covariance between alleles \emph{across} gametes of an individual, i.e., between the maternal and paternal allele:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:D-nongametic}
    D^{(t)}_{k/l} \;:=\; \Cov\big[x^{(m)}_{ik},\, x^{(p)}_{il}\big]
\end{equation}
Since mothers and fathers are exchangeable under \assumptionid{Primary-AM}, swapping $m$ and $p$ does not change the covariance, $\Cov\big[x^{(p)}_{ik},\, x^{(m)}_{il}\big] = D^{(t)}_{k/l}$.

Similarly, we define the \textbf{gametic disequilibrium coefficient}, $D^{(t)}_{kl}$, to be the covariance between alleles within the \emph{same} gamete $u \in \{m,p\}$ of an individual:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:D-gametic}
\begin{aligned}
    D^{(t)}_{kl} \;&:=\; \Cov\big[x^{(u)}_{ik},\, x^{(u)}_{il}\big] \\
    D^{(t)}_{kk} \;&=\; p_k q_k \qquad \text{for all } t
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The second line holds because, when $k = l$, the gametic disequilibrium coefficient is the variance of a single allele, which is fixed for all generations by \assumptionid{Constant-freq}.

We can write the covariance between two genotypes in the same individual, or the variance of a genotype when $k = l$, as the sum of four allelic covariances:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:x-from-D}
\begin{aligned}
    \Cov[\,x_{ik},\, x_{il}\,]
    \;&=\; \sum_{u,v \in \{m,p\}} \Cov\big[x^{(u)}_{ik},\, x^{(v)}_{il}\big] \\
    \;&=\; \Cov\big[x^{(m)}_{ik},\, x^{(m)}_{il}\big] + \Cov\big[x^{(p)}_{ik},\, x^{(p)}_{il}\big] \\
    &\qquad +\; \Cov\big[x^{(m)}_{ik},\, x^{(p)}_{il}\big] + \Cov\big[x^{(p)}_{ik},\, x^{(m)}_{il}\big] \\
    \;&=\; 2\big(D^{(t)}_{kl} + D^{(t)}_{k/l}\big)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The terms and notation used here come from Weir \citep{weir1996book}.

Note that in their work, both coefficients are only defined for different variants, $k \neq l$, while we extend them to the same variant.
When $k=l$, the gametic disequilibrium coefficient becomes the variance of a single allele, and the non-gametic disequilibrium coefficient, $D^{(t)}_{k/k} = \Cov\big[x^{(m)}_{ik},\, x^{(p)}_{ik}\big]$, is what they call the \textbf{Hardy--Weinberg disequilibrium coefficient}.

\paragraph{Allelic marginal effect size}
The third quantity we define is the \textbf{allelic marginal effect size}, i.e., the slope from regressing the phenotype of an individual on one of their alleles, $\gamma_{k}^{(t)}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma-def}
    \gamma^{(t)}_k \;:=\; \frac{\Cov\big[\,x^{(u)}_{ik},\, y_i\,\big]}{\Var\big[\,x^{(u)}_{ik}\,\big]}
\end{equation}

Its numerator decomposes into the sum of covariances between the allele and the causes of the phenotype.
This includes the environmental term (covariance of zero under \assumptionid{GE-joint-indep-within}, which still holds under assortative mating because an individual's own environmental value is drawn independently of their genetics) and each genotype weighted by its effect size.
Each of the latter terms can itself be expanded into an allele on the opposite gamete, $D^{(t)}_{k/l}$, and an allele on the same gamete, $D^{(t)}_{kl}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma-from-D}
\begin{aligned}
    \gamma^{(t)}_k
    \;&=\; \frac{1}{p_k q_k}\Big(\underbrace{\Cov\big[\,x^{(u)}_{ik},\, e_i\,\big]}_{0} + \sum_{l=1}^{M} \beta_l\,\Cov\big[\,x^{(u)}_{ik},\, x_{il}\,\big]\Big) \\
    \;&=\; \frac{1}{p_k q_k} \sum_{l=1}^{M} \beta_l \Big(D^{(t)}_{kl} + D^{(t)}_{k/l}\Big) \\
    \;&=\; \beta_k + \frac{1}{p_k q_k}\Big(\sum_{l \neq k} \beta_l\, D^{(t)}_{kl} + \sum_{l=1}^{M} \beta_l\, D^{(t)}_{k/l}\Big)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The last line separates out the $l = k$ gametic term, $D^{(t)}_{kk} = p_k q_k$, to make its relationship to $\beta_k$ clearer.
A single allele can therefore covary with the phenotype through any variant the individual carries, not just its own.

\paragraph{Additive genetic variance}
Deriving the additive genetic variance, $V_A^{(t)}$, follows similar logic:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:VA-from-gamma}
\begin{aligned}
    \VAt \;=\; \Var[\,g_i\,]
    \;&=\; \sum_{k} \beta_k \Cov[\,x_{ik},\, g_i\,] \\
    \;&=\; \sum_{k} \beta_k \Big(\Cov\big[\,x^{(m)}_{ik},\, y_i\,\big] + \Cov\big[\,x^{(p)}_{ik},\, y_i\,\big]\Big) \\
    \;&=\; \sum_{k} 2 p_k q_k\, \beta_k\, \gamma^{(t)}_k
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The second line swaps $g_i$ for $y_i$, since each allele is uncorrelated with $e_i$ per \assumptionid{GE-joint-indep-within}.
Compared with $\VAo = \sum_k 2 p_k q_k \beta_k^2$, one of the two effect sizes is replaced by the marginal effect size.

\paragraph{Heritability}
Like additive genetic variance, phenotypic variance can also vary across time, $\VYt = \VAt + V_E$ (this formula still holds by \assumptionid{GE-indep-within}).
Similarly, we define generation-specific heritability as follows:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hsq-t}
\begin{aligned}
    \hsqt \;:=\; \frac{\VAt}{\VYt} \;&=\; \frac{\VAt}{\VAt + V_E} \\
    \;&=\; \frac{\sum_k 2 p_k q_k\, \beta_k\, \gamma^{(t)}_k}{\sum_k 2 p_k q_k\, \beta_k\, \gamma^{(t)}_k + V_E}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Genetic value correlation between mates}
We'll also confirm here that $\rho_g^{(t)} = \rho_y V_A^{(t)} / V_Y^{(t)} $ holds for any $t$, rather than just $t=0$ as shown in result~\ref{item:rhog-hsq} of Section~\ref{sec:am-first-gen-mates}.
Per \assumptionid{GE-indep-within}, each mate's genetic value covariance with their phenotype is $\Cov[g_i, y_i] = \Cov[g_i, g_i + e_i] = \Var[g_i] = \VAt$.
Crossing the mating co-path means multiplying these two covariances by $\rho_y / \VYt$ (the denominator is the variance of the variable being matched on, phenotypes):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rhog-t}
\begin{aligned}
    \rhogt \;&=\; \frac{\Cov[\,g_m,\, g_p\,]}{\sqrt{\Var[g_m]\,\Var[g_p]}} \\
    \;&=\; \frac{\overbrace{\Cov[\,g_m,\, y_m\,]}^{\VAt} \cdot\; \dfrac{\rho_y}{\VYt} \;\cdot \overbrace{\Cov[\,y_p,\, g_p\,]}^{\VAt}}{\VAt} \\
    \;&=\; \rho_y\,\frac{\VAt}{\VYt} \;=\; \rho_y\,\hsqt
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The genetic correlation between mates is the phenotypic correlation scaled by that generation's heritability, exactly as it was in $t=0$.

\paragraph{Segregation variation}

Earlier in Section~\ref{sec:transmission-alleles} (Equation~\eqref{eq:allele-transmit-resid}), we parameterized an offspring's allele as the mean of the transmitting parent's two alleles plus an uncorrelated segregation residual.
We can derive the variance of this residual under the presence of correlated parent alleles.
Note our temporary use of the $(t+1)$ superscript on the residual to clarify that the properties of a generation-$t+1$ individual's segregation residual is a function of generation-$t$ properties.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:seg-var-allele}
\begin{aligned}
    \Var\big[x^{\perp(u)\;(t+1)}_{ik}\big]
    \;&=\; \E\Big[\big(T^{(u)}_{ik} - \tfrac12\big)^2\Big]\; \E\Big[\big(x^{(m)}_{u(i),k} - x^{(p)}_{u(i),k}\big)^2\Big] \\
    \;&=\; \tfrac14\Big(p_k q_k + p_k q_k - 2\,D^{(t)}_{k/k}\Big) \\
    \;&=\; \tfrac12\big(p_k q_k - D^{(t)}_{k/k}\big)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
We can also derive the segregation residual of genotypes:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:seg-var-x}
\begin{aligned}
    \Var\big[x^{\perp\;(t+1)}_{ik}\big]
    \;&=\; \Var\big[x^{\perp(m)}_{ik}\big] + \Var\big[x^{\perp(p)}_{ik}\big] + 2\Cov\big[x^{\perp(m)}_{ik},\, x^{\perp(p)}_{ik}\big] \\
    \;&=\; \tfrac12\big(p_k q_k - D^{(t)}_{k/k}\big) + \tfrac12\big(p_k q_k - D^{(t)}_{k/k}\big) + 0 \\
    \;&=\; p_k q_k - D^{(t)}_{k/k}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

These derivations are a generalization of our earlier result in Equation~\eqref{eq:founder-resid-var} that showed that inbred parents pass on alleles with lower segregation variance.
The earlier result only considered IBS between possibly-inherited alleles that arises from IBD, while this section's result encompasses any IBS.

This appendix collects the objects used to describe descent, together with their notation.
They come in three groups.
\emph{Pedigree} objects have individuals as nodes and depend on the pedigree $\Pinfo$ alone.
\emph{Realized allele} objects have alleles as nodes and depend on what was actually transmitted, $\Tinfo$; they differ between variants, and carry the variant index $k$ wherever an allele is written out.
\emph{Possible allele} objects also have alleles as nodes, but depend on $\Pinfo$ alone; they are the same at every variant and carry no $k$.
The terms of path diagrams, Appendix~\ref{app:path-analysis}, are a separate vocabulary and are listed last.

\paragraph{Conventions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A \emph{chain} is a run of single-generation steps of one kind: meioses for lineal and transmission chains, matings for mating chains.
    A \emph{path} joins chains together: a pedigree path alternates lineal and mating chains, and a coalescence path joins two transmission chains.
    \item A single-headed arrow marks a chain that runs from a source to a sink, always from older to younger, and the source is written first: $\Lin(i,j)$, $\Tchain^{(t)}(a)$.
    A double-headed arrow marks an object whose two ends play symmetric roles: $\Mu$, $\PedPath$, $\Cpath$.
    \item A plain $T$ is one realized object; a blackboard $\mathbb{T}$ is the set of possible ones.
    \item A superscript $(t)$ is the generation of the source.
    \item A hat marks either an expectation given the pedigree ($\widehat\Theta_{ij}$, $\widehat{f}_i$, $\widehat\pi_{ij}$) or a most recent quantity, where two lineages meet ($\hat{t}_{ij}$, $\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{ij}$).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{How they relate}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The coalescence graph is the allele-level version of the meiosis graph, and a transmission chain is the allele-level version of a lineal chain.
    \item Dropping the alleles from a possible transmission chain of $\mathring{x}_i^{(u)}$ leaves a lineal chain from an ancestor in generation $t$ down to $i$, whose last meiosis is from $u(i)$.
    Each such lineal chain gives two possible transmission chains, one for each of the ancestor's alleles, which is another way to count them: $2^{t_i - t - 1}$ lineal chains times two alleles is $2^{t_i - t}$.
    \item Dropping the alleles from a possible coalescence path leaves two lineal chains from a common ancestor, which is a pedigree path with no mating chain whenever the two lineal chains share no other individual.
    They can share more: if an ancestor between them is inbred, the two transmission chains can pass through that ancestor's two different alleles.
    This is why ``share no other allele'' is a rule about alleles rather than individuals, and why the allele vocabulary is kept separate from the pedigree one.
    \item Every realized object is one of the possible ones: $\Tchain^{(t)}(\mathring{x}_{ik}^{(u)}) \in \Tset^{(t)}(\mathring{x}_i^{(u)})$, and every recent coalescence path is an element of $\Cset$.
    \item Under \assumptionid{Unrelated-mates} and \assumptionid{Max-MRCAs}, the possible coalescence paths of $i$ and $j$ correspond one-to-one to the alleles of their MRCAs: $|\Cset(i,j)| = 2|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{ij}|$, each of length $(t_i - \hat{t}_{ij}) + (t_j - \hat{t}_{ij})$, Equation~\eqref{eq:well-behaved-cpaths}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Path analysis}\label{app:path-analysis}

Path analysis is a tool for modeling the covariance between variables in a causal system, such as genetic inheritance and assortative mating.
Path analysis was introduced by Sewall Wright \citep{Wright1921JAgricRes}, as a way of decomposing an observed correlation between variables into the contributions of a hypothesized causal structure, and set out systematically thirteen years later as the method of path coefficients \citep{Wright1934AnnMathStat}.
Wright applied it to mating systems in the same period \citep{Wright1921GeneticsMatingI, Wright1921GeneticsMatingIII}; together with Fisher's treatment of the same problem \citep{Fisher1918TransRoySocEdin}, that work is the origin of the classical results on how assortment changes the correlation between relatives.
The explanations below are adapted from the Supplementary Note of \citet{Sunde2025NatComm}, which introduced the co-path edge type (described below).
Unlike \citet{Sunde2025NatComm}, we do not assume that the variables are standardized, so we keep track of variances explicitly.

A path diagram is a graphical representation of a generative model.
Each variable is a node (drawn as a rectangle if it is observed and an ellipse if it is latent), and the nodes are joined by three kinds of edges:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A \textbf{directed} edge $a \rightarrow b$ labeled with a coefficient $c$ says that $a$ is a cause of $b$, contributing $c\,a$ to $b$'s structural equation.
    Collecting the arrows that point \emph{into} a node recovers that node's equation, so the diagram and the model equations carry the same content.
    \item A \textbf{bidirected} edge $a \leftrightarrow b$ carries covariance between the \emph{disturbances} of $a$ and $b$.
    A variable's disturbance is what remains of it once its causes in the diagram are accounted for: if the arrows into $b$ give $b = c_1 a_1 + \cdots + c_k a_k + \epsilon_b$, then $\epsilon_b$ is $b$'s disturbance, and for a variable with no incoming arrows the disturbance is the variable itself.
    The self-loop $a \leftrightarrow a$ is the case $b=a$: it is $\Var[\epsilon_a]$, the part of $\Var[a]$ not supplied by $a$'s causes, so a variable with no incoming arrows carries its entire variance on such a loop.
    \item A \textbf{co-path} $a \;\text{---}\; b$, drawn as a plain line with no arrowheads, is covariance attributable to \emph{matching}: $a$ and $b$ came to be associated because they were paired on their values, not because they share a cause.
\end{itemize}

        }
        \label{fig:path-diagram-example}
    \end{floatcard}
\end{figure}

The covariance between \emph{any} two variables in the diagram, latent or observed, can be calculated quickly by following Wright's tracing rules.
Enumerate every \emph{valid chain} joining the two variables; their covariance is the sum over chains of the product of the coefficients each chain passes through.
A valid chain between $a$ and $b$ has the shape
$$
    a \leftarrow \cdots \leftarrow u \;\leftrightarrow\; v \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow b :
$$
trace \emph{backward} along directed edges from $a$ to some ancestor $u$, cross \emph{exactly one} bidirected edge $u \leftrightarrow v$ (possibly a self-loop, $u=v$), then trace \emph{forward} along directed edges from $v$ down to $b$.
A chain turns around exactly once, and it turns around on a bidirected edge, because that is the only place variance enters the system.

Co-paths extend this.
A chain may pass \emph{through} a co-path and keep going: a co-path between matched variables $a'$ and $b'$ joins two valid chains of the kind above into a longer one,
$$
    \underbrace{a \leftarrow \cdots \leftrightarrow \cdots \rightarrow a'}_{\text{valid chain}}
    \;\text{---}\;
    \underbrace{b' \leftarrow \cdots \leftrightarrow \cdots \rightarrow b}_{\text{valid chain}} .
$$
Two further rules apply \citep{Sunde2025NatComm}: a chain can neither start nor end with a co-path, and a chain cannot use two co-paths belonging to the same mating process.
It \emph{may} cross co-paths of different mating processes.

A bidirected edge is a statement about disturbances, so a chain terminates on it: a disturbance has no causes in the diagram, so there is nothing to keep tracing back through, and the chain cannot reach past $a'$ to the things that caused $a'$.
A co-path is a statement about the matched variables themselves, so a chain continues backward through it, which encodes the fact that matching two individuals on some variable induces correlation between \emph{all the causes} of that variable.

The quantity multiplied along a chain when it crosses a co-path between $a'$ and $b'$ is not their correlation $\rho$ itself, but
\begin{equation}\label{eq:copath-mu}
    \mu \;:=\; \frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\Var[a']\,\Var[b']}} ,
\end{equation}
because a co-path with coefficient $\mu$ contributes $\Cov[a',b'] = \mu\,\Var[a']\,\Var[b']$ \citep{Sunde2025NatComm}.
The two coincide only when the matched variables have unit variance.
If the variances of the matched variables change, for example from one generation to the next, a value of $\mu$ carried over unchanged is therefore wrong, which is the practical reason we label a co-path with its correlation in brackets, as $[\rho]$, and leave $\mu$ to be derived from the diagram's own variances.

Tracing chains by hand is instructive for a single pair and hopeless for a pedigree, since the number of chains grows multiplicatively with depth.
The diagrams and covariances in this document were produced with \texttt{pathMgr}, a small package written alongside it: it takes a model specification, returns the covariance between any two of its variables as an algebraic expression, and emits the path diagram to go with it.
It computes each covariance in two independent ways, by enumerating chains exactly as above and in closed form from the matrix identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ram}
    \bSigma \;=\; \B{F}(\bI-\B{B})^{-1}\bS(\bI-\B{B})^{-\intercal}\B{F}^{\intercal}
\end{equation}
and their agreement is the correctness check.

That identity is the diagram written as matrices, one row and column per variable.
Collecting all the variables into a vector $\bv$, the matrix $\B{B}$ holds the directed edges ($B_{ij}$ is the coefficient on the arrow from $j$ to $i$), so the whole system of structural equations is $\bv = \B{B}\bv + \bepsilon$, where $\bepsilon$ is the vector of disturbances.
The symmetric matrix $\bS$ holds the bidirected edges: $S_{ij}$ is the value of $i \leftrightarrow j$, and its diagonal $S_{ii}$ is the self-loop, the variance of $i$'s disturbance.
$\B{F}$ merely selects rows, keeping the variables we want reported and discarding the rest.

The inverse is what does the tracing.
In an acyclic model $\B{B}$ is nilpotent, so $(\bI-\B{B})^{-1} = \bI + \B{B} + \B{B}^2 + \cdots$, and the $(i,u)$ entry of that sum is the total over all directed paths from $u$ to $i$ of the product of their coefficients, with one term per path and $\B{B}^n$ contributing the paths of length $n$.
Sandwiching $\bS$ between it and its transpose therefore gives, for latent and observed variables alike,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ram-trace}
    \Sigma_{ij} \;=\; \sum_{u}\sum_{v}
    \underbrace{\big[(\bI-\B{B})^{-1}\big]_{iu}}_{i \leftarrow \cdots \leftarrow u}
    \underbrace{S_{uv}}_{u \leftrightarrow v}
    \underbrace{\big[(\bI-\B{B})^{-1}\big]_{jv}}_{v \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow j} ,
\end{equation}
which is Wright's rule term for term: a backward leg, exactly one bidirected edge, a forward leg, summed over every chain, indexed by the $u \leftrightarrow v$ it turns around on.
The two methods are the same statement (one enumerated, one summed in closed form), which is why their agreement tests the implementation rather than the mathematics.
Co-paths are the one thing that sits outside this identity and contributes further terms; there, the chain rules given above govern them.
